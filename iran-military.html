Iran's Military Position

Heavily damaged conventionally; still able to impose costs through dispersed systems and geography
Current assessment

U.S. and Israeli operations have destroyed or damaged important Iranian command, air-defense, missile, drone, naval, and logistics infrastructure. They have not disarmed Iran. Renewed attacks on shipping and regional targets in July show that Iran still retains enough missiles, drones, coastal systems, small craft, mines, and command capacity to disrupt commerce and sustain a coercive campaign. The central uncertainty is no longer whether Iran was damaged, but how quickly that damage translates into reduced operational effect.

What the evidence supports

Established

  • Air and naval access: U.S. forces can conduct repeated strikes across Iran and along the southern coast with little evidence of an effective Iranian manned-air response.
  • Major physical damage: imagery, official releases, and specialist analysis support extensive damage to air defenses, command facilities, naval sites, missile and drone infrastructure, and logistics.
  • Continuing Iranian fire: Iran has continued launching missiles and drones and attacking commercial vessels after multiple strike waves.
  • Surviving maritime threat: coastal launchers, mines, drones, small craft, and the narrow geography of Hormuz remain relevant even after losses to larger vessels.

Not established

  • A complete loss ledger: public totals are dominated by U.S., Israeli, or Iranian claims and often mix struck, damaged, and destroyed targets.
  • A remaining inventory: subtracting claimed launches and losses from old stockpile estimates ignores hidden stocks, failures, production, decoys, and category differences.
  • Permanent suppression: a destroyed fixed site does not prove that mobile or redundant capacity is gone.
  • Strategic defeat: operational dominance has not yet produced a stable maritime order, a verified nuclear settlement, or Iranian political capitulation.

Evidence: CENTCOM Operation Epic Fury fact sheet; CSIS military reconstitution study; RAND war-termination assessment.

The force that remains

Most degraded

  • Integrated air defense: fixed radars, batteries, and command nodes have been repeatedly targeted, allowing sustained U.S. operations.
  • Manned air power: aircraft and bases have been struck, while Iran's observable retaliation relies overwhelmingly on missiles and drones.
  • Large naval platforms: conventional ships, bases, maintenance infrastructure, and IRGC naval facilities have suffered heavy losses.
  • Centralized command: leadership deaths and attacks on headquarters disrupted the prewar structure, even though the state reconstituted a chain of authority.

Most resilient

  • Mobile missiles and drones: dispersed launchers, storage, simpler production, and preplanned targeting make complete suppression difficult.
  • Littoral coercion: mines, coastal missiles, one-way drones, small craft, and ambiguous attribution can suppress shipping without fleet control.
  • Cyber and intelligence tools: physical strikes do not automatically remove access already established in foreign networks.
  • Partner forces: Hezbollah, Iraqi groups, and the Houthis retain different levels of autonomy and capability; they are not a single centrally controlled arsenal.

Command, adaptation, and reconstitution

Mojtaba Khamenei's selection as supreme leader restored a formal apex after the opening decapitation strikes, but the internal balance remains opaque. CTP–ISW assesses that IRGC commander Ahmad Vahidi and a circle of senior Guard figures have gained influence around the new leader. That is a reasoned assessment from reporting and observable appointments, not proof that the constitutional system has been replaced by a junta.

Iran's recovery will be uneven. Large ships, specialized radars, advanced air-defense components, and complex missile-production machinery are slow and visible to replace. Simple drones, mobile launch practices, underground storage, commercial electronics, and dual-use machinery are easier to disperse. CSIS identifies Chinese, Pakistani, and Caspian procurement routes as important reconstitution channels, while also emphasizing uncertainty about the underlying damage.

The right test

Do not ask only how many systems Iran has left. Ask whether it can find targets, communicate orders, generate repeated salvos, penetrate defenses, repair launch networks, protect production, and impose enough risk to change another actor's behavior.

Nuclear-adjacent military questions

Strike damage at nuclear-related sites should not be folded into a conventional battle-damage total. The IAEA cannot currently account for the size, composition, or whereabouts of Iran's enriched-uranium stockpile or verify whether enrichment-related activity has stopped. Pickaxe Mountain, near Natanz, has not been inspected; its present contents are not publicly established.

Standing assessment: physical hardening and deeper underground construction can raise the cost of attack, but neither visible construction nor a successful strike proves what material, machinery, or activity is present.

Evidence: IAEA safeguards findings reported by AP; AP on Pickaxe Mountain.

What would change this assessment

  • A sustained fall in Iranian launch tempo that cannot be explained by political restraint.
  • Independent imagery showing repeated failure to repair coastal, missile, drone, and command networks.
  • Verified loss or recovery of key production machinery and imported components.
  • Evidence that orders are no longer reaching field units, or that units are operating outside central control.
  • A maritime agreement that produces durable commercial normalization rather than a short pause in attacks.

Losses and Battle Damage

What is supported, what is claimed, and what the public record cannot count
A ledger with confidence levels, not a scoreboard

“Targeted,” “struck,” “damaged,” “destroyed,” and “made inoperable” are different findings. The entries below avoid a single grand total because public reporting combines different dates, equipment classes, and belligerent assessments. A precise number would imply a level of knowledge the evidence does not provide.

Iranian losses

HIGH CONFIDENCE — NAVAL INFRASTRUCTURE AND PLATFORMS
U.S. strikes have repeatedly hit large vessels, naval bases, maintenance facilities, small craft, coastal surveillance, and maritime command infrastructure. The public evidence supports heavy degradation, but not the blanket claim that both Iranian navies are operationally eliminated. July attacks on shipping are direct evidence that the wider maritime threat survived.
HIGH CONFIDENCE — MANNED AIR POWER DEGRADED
Aircraft, runways, shelters, and support infrastructure have been struck. Iran has shown little observable manned-air capacity against repeated U.S. operations. The exact number of aircraft destroyed is not independently established and matters less than the force's demonstrated inability to contest the air campaign.
HIGH CONFIDENCE — AIR-DEFENSE NETWORK DISRUPTED
Fixed radars, batteries, command sites, and surveillance nodes have been repeatedly targeted. Sustained stand-in strikes support the judgment that the integrated network is severely degraded. It does not follow that every mobile battery, short-range system, or local threat has been destroyed.
MEDIUM CONFIDENCE — MISSILE AND DRONE CAPACITY REDUCED
Launchers, storage, production machinery, tunnel entrances, and command nodes have been struck. Iran's continuing launches demonstrate that capacity remains. No public source can convert claimed target counts into a reliable estimate of the launchers, missiles, or drones still operational.
HIGH CONFIDENCE — LEADERSHIP AND COMMAND LOSSES
The opening campaign killed Ali Khamenei and other senior figures and damaged major headquarters. Iran nevertheless selected a successor, restored public command structures, negotiated, and continued operations. Command was disrupted; state authority did not disappear.
CONFIRMED DAMAGE — DUAL-USE AND CIVILIAN INFRASTRUCTURE
Fuel, transport, electricity, water, port, and industrial systems have been damaged alongside military infrastructure. These effects can hinder operations and reconstruction, but they also create civilian harm and cannot be counted as military-equipment losses. OCHA and WHO document extensive impacts on essential services.

U.S. and coalition losses and costs

AIRCRAFT AND UNCREWED SYSTEMS
Public reporting records aircraft and drone losses, damage, and high-risk recovery operations, but available totals mix destroyed, damaged, combat, accident, and uncrewed systems. Use incident-level reporting; do not publish a single aggregate without the underlying congressional or Defense Department record.
PERSONNEL
U.S. service members have been killed and wounded in Iranian attacks and operational incidents, including new fatalities during the July escalation. Because official totals change and may use different inclusion rules, the report will cite dated incident or Defense Department totals rather than carry forward an undated cumulative number.
FINANCIAL AND READINESS COST
The campaign consumes strike weapons, air-defense interceptors, flight hours, sealift, maintenance, and emergency funding. Supplemental requests are not identical to costs already incurred. Any published cost must separate prior expenditure, replacement cost, requested authority, and spending for unrelated priorities.

How to read later updates

  • Official claim: useful for target category and intent, not independent proof of effect.
  • Imagery-confirmed: visible physical damage at a specific place and date; hidden and functional effects may remain unknown.
  • Operationally demonstrated: the strongest evidence of current capacity is repeated observed use or inability to use it.
  • Estimated: an analytical reconstruction that must state assumptions and cannot be silently promoted to fact.

How Iran Imposes Costs

A layered coercive system, not a single missile inventory

Iran's response combines military effects with economic and political pressure. It does not need to defeat the United States conventionally. It needs to preserve enough uncertainty, attack capacity, and escalation options to make shipping, basing, reconstruction, and continued operations more costly than its opponents are willing to bear.

Direct tools

  • Ballistic and cruise missiles: higher-speed attacks against bases, air defenses, infrastructure, and cities.
  • One-way attack drones: cheaper volume, route flexibility, and pressure on expensive interceptor inventories.
  • Coastal and maritime systems: anti-ship missiles, drones, mines, small craft, boarding, and route coercion.
  • Cyber operations: espionage, disruption, and latent access that can outlast damage to physical platforms.

Indirect tools

  • Partner forces: Hezbollah, Iraqi groups, and the Houthis create additional fronts but retain different interests and autonomy.
  • Infrastructure pressure: attacks on energy, water, ports, airports, and commercial centers spread the cost to regional governments.
  • Commercial risk: a small number of attacks can deter far more vessels through insurance, crewing, and liability effects.
  • Negotiating linkage: Tehran connects Hormuz, sanctions, the blockade, the nuclear file, and Lebanon to preserve leverage across tracks.
Doctrine versus available capacity

Prewar doctrine describes options; observed behavior shows what was selected under real constraints. Non-use does not prove that a weapon is unavailable, and an old inventory does not prove that it is operational. The strongest indicators are repeated launch generation, target selection, penetration, damage, and the ability to resume operations after strikes.

The strategic reserve problem

Iran has incentives to withhold some systems, disperse them, or use them only when political value exceeds exposure risk. Analysts therefore cannot infer the exact reserve from launch tempo alone. Conversely, claims about untouched “strategic reserves” are also speculative unless supported by observable deployment, production, or use.

Analysis: Carnegie on Iran's adapted strategy; RAND's dated March forecast; RUSI on loitering munitions.

What Iran Has Actually Done

Observed wartime behavior from the opening salvos through the July relapse

The pattern across the war

Iran's behavior has evolved through four overlapping stages: broad regional retaliation after the 28 February opening strikes; sustained pressure on Gulf infrastructure and shipping; coercive activity during ceasefires and negotiations; and renewed attacks on vessels and regional targets during the July breakdown. The pattern matters more than a single cumulative projectile count.

Observed choices

  • Missile and drone attacks on U.S., Israeli, and Gulf military targets.
  • Attacks on airports, energy, desalination, electricity, and commercial infrastructure, spreading exposure beyond belligerent forces.
  • Repeated attacks, threats, and route demands directed at commercial shipping in and around Hormuz.
  • Use of pauses and negotiations without surrendering the ability to resume coercive operations.

What those choices show

  • Iran can still generate attacks after extensive strikes on launch and command infrastructure.
  • Accuracy and penetration are uneven, but limited physical success can still create large commercial and political effects.
  • Gulf states are both security partners of the United States and exposed pressure points.
  • Partner-force activity is coordinated in some cases but should not be described as automatic remote control from Tehran.

The July evidence

After attacks on commercial vessels from 7 July, CENTCOM resumed repeated strikes against Iranian air defenses, coastal radar, missile and drone capabilities, command sites, logistics, and small craft. Iran answered with attacks on U.S. and regional targets and continued maritime pressure. By 20–22 July, the exchange had become a sustained reprisal cycle and included civilian and critical infrastructure.

Current evidence: CENTCOM, 13 July; AP, 22 July; CSIS, 20 July.

What cannot be inferred

Launches do not reveal the remaining stockpile; failed attacks do not make the coercive strategy irrelevant; and a decline in tempo may reflect depletion, concealment, tactical pause, political restraint, or target scarcity. As of July 29, 2026, the public evidence does not distinguish cleanly among those explanations.

Hormuz: Passage Is Not Normalization

The military channel, commercial market, and political order are three different problems
Current status

Ships can pass through the Strait of Hormuz, but traffic is again suppressed by attacks, renewed U.S. blockade measures, route disputes, and uncertainty after the June memorandum began to break down. Iran does not need to seal the channel physically to disrupt it: repeated attacks, mines, route demands, and the threat of escalation can deter owners, crews, insurers, and charterers.

Four layers of the situation

Security

  • Physical passage: available, but incidents and military operations can interrupt it quickly.
  • Attack capacity: coastal missiles, drones, mines, small craft, and ambiguous launch points remain difficult to eliminate completely.
  • Protection: escorts and strikes can reduce risk for selected movements but do not guarantee the whole commercial system.

Commerce and politics

  • Commercial appetite: insurance, crewing, liability, freight rates, and port confidence determine whether passage is usable at scale.
  • Routing: Iran has sought recognition of an Iranian-managed route; the United States and Gulf partners reject coercive control.
  • Political authority: the June memorandum did not settle who may administer services, impose conditions, or guarantee navigation.

Why naval destruction did not solve the problem

Large platforms are visible and vulnerable. Mines, mobile coastal systems, one-way drones, small boats, and hidden command networks are cheaper, easier to disperse, and well suited to a narrow waterway. IISS and RUSI therefore reach a similar operational conclusion: military action can destroy systems and shape risk, but attack cessation and broad commercial confidence ultimately require a political arrangement that Iran's command structure observes.

“Open” has a checklist

For the report to call Hormuz normalized, traffic volumes and energy flows must recover, attacks must stop, mines and navigational interference must be controlled, insurance and crewing must return, ports and refineries must operate, and the rules of passage must be predictable and accepted.

Analysis: IISS; RUSI; CFR.

What to watch

  • Independent traffic and cargo-volume data by route, vessel class, and direction.
  • War-risk coverage, crew restrictions, freight rates, and owner guidance.
  • Verified mine-clearance activity and navigational-interference reports.
  • Whether attacks cease across multiple weeks rather than during a short negotiating pause.
  • Any published agreement defining navigation, fees, enforcement, the U.S. blockade, and dispute resolution.
