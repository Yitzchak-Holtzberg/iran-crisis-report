Expert Analysis: What Happens Next

Thematic synthesis from CSIS, ISW, Carnegie, Brookings, Atlantic Council, CFR, RAND, JINSA & Alma Center
Updated Mar 22 — Week 4, Day 24
MAJOR: Iran launches long‑range strikes on southern Israel; Tehran and Natanz struck — IAEA investigating Natanz

On Mar 21–22 Iran launched ballistic missile strikes that hit towns near Israel's Dimona nuclear research area (Arad and Dimona), causing mass casualties and widespread damage; emergency services reported well over 100 wounded. Israeli and U.S. forces have simultaneously expanded strikes into Tehran and across multiple provinces in Iran. The Natanz enrichment site was reported damaged and the IAEA has opened inquiries while saying it detected no rise in radiation levels outside the facility. Iran also launched missiles toward Diego Garcia; officials reported the base was not struck. — Source: IAEA; Reuters; BBC; The New York Times; Mar 21–22

ENERGY/DIPLOMACY: U.S. temporarily waives sanctions on Iranian oil at sea

The U.S. issued a short‑term waiver (30 days) permitting the sale of Iranian oil already loaded on tankers to ease acute market pressure caused by the Strait of Hormuz disruption; estimates place the volume of oil at sea in the hundreds of millions of barrels. This is an emergency market step that could stabilise prices briefly but may complicate longer‑term pressure on Tehran. — Source: Reuters; New York Times; Mar 20–21

REGIONAL REACTIONS: Saudi Arabia expels Iranian military attache; Gulf diplomatic pressure increases

Saudi Arabia declared Iran's military attache and several embassy staff persona non grata on Mar 21 amid a broader wave of Gulf diplomatic measures following repeated Iranian strikes on Gulf states and regional infrastructure. Other Gulf capitals have signalled tougher posture on Hormuz security. — Source: Reuters; Mar 21

Operation Epic Fury continues to impose heavy costs on Iran and the global economy. Through Mar 22 coalition and Israeli air operations have struck thousands of regime, military, and dual‑use targets; public tallies vary (CENTCOM has cited 5,500+ strikes in some statements while senior U.S. commanders have at times referred to higher aggregated target counts). Recent days saw Iranian ballistic strikes that hit towns near Israel's Dimona nuclear research center (resulting in well over 100 wounded), reported Israeli strikes on Tehran, and damage reported at the Natanz enrichment site (IAEA investigating; no detectable radiation rise reported externally). Iran also launched missiles toward Diego Garcia (officials say the base was not hit). The U.S. moved to ease an immediate energy shock by temporarily waiving sanctions on some Iranian oil at sea, while regional tensions rose as Saudi Arabia expelled Iranian diplomatic staff. The campaign continues to degrade Iranian conventional forces, but kinetic pressure has also raised nuclear, humanitarian, and economic escalation risks. — Source: IAEA; Reuters; Washington Post; New York Times; Mar 20–22

Converging assessments

  • Iran's conventional military capability and missile inventories have been significantly degraded by sustained strikes, limiting but not eliminating asymmetric retaliation options.
  • Mojtaba Khamenei remains effectively unseen publicly; the IRGC is increasingly central to governance and operational decision‑making.
  • Strait of Hormuz traffic remains highly constrained with insurance and commercial withdrawals; the U.S. waiver on Iranian oil at sea is an emergency market measure.
  • IAEA investigations into the Natanz incident underscore growing nuclear verification and material‑security risks even where no external radiation rise is detected.
  • Diplomatic and fiscal pressures (sanctions waivers, coalition assurances for Hormuz) complicate strategic calculations for both the U.S. and Iran.

Key disagreements

  • Whether leadership decapitation will fragment or harden IRGC control.
  • How to secure unaccounted enriched uranium stockpiles without causing a nuclear incident — covert seizure options remain debated.
  • Whether the U.S. can sustain munitions and logistics for continued high‑tempo operations without major supplemental appropriations.
  • Whether a calibrated diplomatic off‑ramp still exists given reciprocal strikes on nuclear and energy infrastructure.
  • Immediate test: whether the regime can manage payroll and public order through Nawruz and the coming weeks.

Military Operations & Targeting

CSIS ISW JINSA Alma CFR Brookings

CENTCOM and coalition strikes have hit thousands of targets across Iran, including missile production, command nodes, and transport hubs; public counts vary by source. The IDF has expanded operations into Tehran and other deep targets. Iran continues to employ missiles and drones regionally — notably the Mar 21–22 ballistic strikes that impacted towns near Israel's Dimona facility. The Natanz incident has prompted IAEA inquiries. — CSIS; ISW; Reuters; IAEA; Mar 21–22

Where they agree
  • Iran's missile force is heavily degraded — pre‑war inventories and launcher availability have been sharply reduced; production bottlenecks limit near‑term replenishment. — ISW; CSIS; Mar 12–22
  • Coalition air and maritime superiority — coalition and Israeli forces report freedom to strike deep targets across multiple provinces and maritime choke points. — CSIS; CENTCOM; Mar 12–22
  • Drone strikes remain Iran's most persistent asymmetric tool — drones account for the majority of Iran‑attributable attacks on shipping and regional infrastructure despite efforts to interdict them. — CSIS; Mar 8–22
Where they differ
  • Nuclear threat horizon: Some analysts see kinetic damage to enrichment sites as limiting short‑term breakout risk; others warn that facility strikes complicate verification and could increase proliferation risk if material is dispersed or hidden. — CSIS; IAEA; Mar 21–22
  • Sustainment: Debate persists over U.S. munitions stocks and budgetary sufficiency for a prolonged high‑tempo campaign; observers expect supplemental requests if operations continue. — CSIS; Brookings; Mar 11–21
  • Russian/Chinese assistance: Open‑source reporting raises questions about materiel or intelligence flows to Iran; assessments vary in confidence and require continued monitoring. — ISW; open reporting, Mar 14–22

Succession & Governance

Carnegie CFR RAND Brookings

Mojtaba Khamenei was selected as supreme leader on Mar 8 after his father was killed in the Feb 28 opening strikes. He has made few or no public appearances since selection, fueling questions about his health and the balance of power; allied assessments indicate the IRGC is exercising outsized influence over governance and military decisions as the kinetic campaign continues. — Carnegie; Brookings; Mar 8–22

Where they agree
  • Dynastic succession contradicts revolutionary principles but was the only viable option — Khamenei had pre‑stacked key institutions with loyalists; wartime conditions limited alternatives. — Carnegie; Brookings, Mar 6–11
  • No large‑scale security defections reported — security forces remain largely intact and loyal; elite defections would be the decisive indicator for regime fracture. — Brookings; Carnegie, Mar 11–22
  • If war ends now, Iran likely survives in a weakened but repressive form — survival, not victory, is the low bar that might constitute a political win for Tehran. — Brookings; Carnegie, Mar 10–21
Where they differ
  • Post‑war governance: CFR and other analysts differ on whether a managed transition ("Islamic Republic 2.0") is feasible or whether prolonged kinetic pressure will harden IRGC rule. — CFR; RAND; Mar 11–22
  • Proxy viability: RAND sees proxy financing and sustainment degrading quicker than Tehran can adapt; Carnegie argues endurance doctrine preserves proxy utility longer. — RAND; Carnegie, Mar 2026

RAND Scenario Status Check

  • Suppression + Succession — dominant trajectory with Mojtaba installed and IRGC managing governance.
  • Popular Uprising — January protests were crushed and large‑scale elite defections have not materialized; this scenario remains possible but low probability without a major security split.
  • Forward defense doctrine facing strain — proxy networks and strategic depth are under pressure from coalition strikes and financial constraints. — RAND, Mar 2026

Carnegie: The Endurance Strategy

  • "Iran does not need to win — it needs to survive" (Grajewski). Survival under intense pressure is a plausible outcome that shapes Tehran's choices. — Carnegie; Mar 11–22
  • China's posture is transactional — Beijing maintains ties but avoids overt military rescue; economic lifelines are limited in scope. — Carnegie (Feigenbaum), Mar 2–22
  • Most likely outcome: a weakened Islamic Republic capable of ruling a devastated society, perhaps with increased repression and IRGC dominance. — Carnegie; Brookings, Mar 11–22

Energy, Maritime & Regional Spillover

CSIS CFR RAND Atl. Council Brookings Carnegie JINSA

The Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed to most commercial traffic; the halt to tanker flows and insurance withdrawals have produced a severe energy shock. Brent and other benchmarks spiked after strikes on South Pars and Ras Laffan; the U.S. waiver on Iranian oil at sea is an emergency measure to relieve market pressure. The widening theater — including attacks on a Qatari gas hub reported by multiple outlets — increases the risk of prolonged disruption to global energy and some high‑tech supply chains. — Reuters; CSIS; Atlantic Council; Mar 20–22

Where they agree
  • Economic leverage through geography — Iran's ability to disrupt Hormuz imposes outsized costs on global markets and forces urgent policy tradeoffs for consumer economies. — Atlantic Council; Brookings; Mar 12–22
  • Energy market interventions — the U.S. waiver on some Iranian oil was explicitly designed to steady markets in the near term; its longer‑term strategic consequences are contested. — Reuters; New York Times; Mar 20–21
  • Gulf states moving from hedging to containment — diplomatic expulsions and limited kinetic responses signal Gulf states are increasingly willing to take punitive measures against Iran. — Reuters; Mar 21
Where they differ
  • Houthi activation risk: Some analysts warn Houthis could open a second major transit choke point in the Red Sea; others note operational constraints and competing incentives that may keep them quiescent. — CSIS; Atlantic Council, Mar 16–21
  • Duration of closure: Predictions vary on how long insurers will refuse Gulf war‑risk cover and how quickly traffic can normalize even after a pause in hostilities. — Brookings; Reuters, Mar 20–22

Civilian Impact & Accountability

CSIS Brookings CFR Alma Carnegie

Humanitarian needs continue to escalate: displacement, food and medicine shortages, and infrastructure damage are acute across Iran, Lebanon, and parts of Iraq. Reports of executions and harsh crackdowns persist inside Iran. The strike damage in Israeli towns and the risk to nuclear facilities heighten both civilian‑protection concerns and the potential for legal and accountability investigations. — UNHCR; Washington Post; AP; Mar 21–22

Where they agree
  • Escalating humanitarian toll — displacement, shortages, and civilian casualties are mounting; independent accountability mechanisms remain essential. — UN; Brookings; Mar 10–22
  • Nuclear‑site strikes raise unique risks — any kinetic damage to enrichment facilities amplifies risks of contamination, operational failures, and verification blind spots. — IAEA; Washington Post; Mar 21–22
Where they differ
  • Operational constraints: Some policymakers argue civilian‑casualty scrutiny will constrain future strikes; others believe transparency and legal scrutiny will not meaningfully slow kinetic operations in the near term. — Brookings; CSIS; Mar 11–22
