Expert Analysis: What Happens Next

Thematic synthesis from CSIS, ISW, Carnegie, Brookings, Atlantic Council, CFR, RAND, JINSA & Alma Center
Updated Mar 21 — Week 4, Day 23
MAJOR: Strikes on Tehran, Beirut; Natanz Reportedly Hit — IAEA Investigating

Coalition and Israeli airstrikes on Mar 21 included reported strikes in Tehran and southern Beirut, and Iran reported renewed damage at its Natanz enrichment site; the IAEA has opened inquiries into the Natanz report amid continuing concern about nuclear‑material security. Iran also launched ballistic missiles toward Diego Garcia (officials say the base was not struck). These developments mark a further expansion of strike corridors and elevated nuclear risk. — Source: Reuters; Washington Post; IAEA; NBC News, Mar 21

ENERGY/DIPLOMACY: U.S. Temporarily Waives Sanctions on Iranian Oil at Sea

On Mar 20 the U.S. issued a 30‑day waiver allowing the sale of Iranian oil already at sea (estimated ~140 million barrels) to ease global energy supply strains caused by the Strait of Hormuz disruption. The move is intended to calm markets but risks providing Tehran limited liquidity unless further restrictions on receipts are enforced. — Source: Reuters; New York Times; Mar 20

REGIONAL REACTIONS: Saudi Arabia Expels Iranian Military Attache, Gulf Pressure Rises

Saudi Arabia declared Iran's military attache and several embassy staff persona non grata on Mar 21, reflecting rising Gulf diplomatic retaliation after repeated Iranian strikes on Gulf states and infrastructure. Several other regional diplomatic measures and coalition statements on Hormuz security have followed. — Source: Reuters, Mar 21

Operation Epic Fury continues to impose heavy costs on Iran and the global economy. Through Mar 21 the campaign has struck hundreds to thousands of regime, military, and dual‑use targets: CENTCOM and coalition strikes remain intensive and the IDF has expanded operations into new strike lanes. Recent days saw reported Israeli strikes on Tehran and Lebanese Hezbollah positions, renewed reports of damage at the Natanz enrichment site (IAEA investigating), and Iranian ballistic missiles launched toward Diego Garcia (authorities report the base was not hit). The U.S. moved to ease an immediate energy shock by temporarily waiving sanctions on some Iranian oil at sea, while regional tensions rose as Saudi Arabia expelled Iranian diplomatic staff. The campaign continues to degrade Iranian conventional forces, but kinetic pressure has also raised nuclear, humanitarian, and economic escalation risks. — Source: Reuters; Washington Post; IAEA; New York Times; Mar 20–21

Converging assessments

  • Iran's conventional military severely degraded — but targeted leadership killings and facility strikes are eroding command cohesion at the top
  • Mojtaba Khamenei remains effectively unseen publicly; the IRGC is increasingly central to governance and operational decision‑making
  • Strait of Hormuz traffic remains near‑zero and global LNG flows disrupted; the U.S. waiver on Iranian oil at sea is an emergency market measure
  • IAEA investigations into Natanz underscore growing nuclear verification and material‑security risks
  • Diplomatic and fiscal pressures (sanctions waivers, coalition assurances for Hormuz) complicate strategic calculations for both the U.S. and Iran

Key disagreements

  • Whether leadership decapitation will fragment or harden IRGC control
  • How to secure unaccounted enriched uranium stockpiles without causing a nuclear incident — covert seizure options remain debated
  • Whether the U.S. can sustain munitions and logistics for continued high‑tempo operations without major supplemental appropriations
  • Whether a calibrated diplomatic off‑ramp still exists given reciprocal strikes on nuclear and energy infrastructure
  • Immediate test: whether the regime can manage payroll and public order through Nawruz and the coming weeks

Military Operations & Targeting

CSIS ISW JINSA Alma CFR Brookings

CENTCOM has struck 5,500+ targets including 60+ ships; IDF has conducted thousands of strikes across Iran. Iranian ballistic missile launches have dropped markedly since Day 1, but Iran continues to employ missiles and drones regionally. Recent reported strikes on Tehran and Natanz raise the conflict's geographic and strategic scope. — CSIS; ISW; Reuters; Washington Post, Mar 21

Where they agree
  • Iran's missile force is heavily degraded — pre‑war inventories and launcher availability have been sharply reduced; production bottlenecks limit near‑term replenishment. — ISW; CSIS; Mar 12–21
  • Coalition air and maritime superiority — coalition and Israeli forces report freedom to strike deep targets across multiple provinces and maritime choke points. — CSIS; CENTCOM; Mar 12–21
  • Drone strikes remain Iran's most persistent asymmetric tool — drones account for the majority of Iran‑attributable attacks on shipping and regional infrastructure despite efforts to interdict them. — CSIS; Mar 8–21
Where they differ
  • Nuclear threat horizon: Some analysts see kinetic damage to enrichment sites as limiting short‑term breakout risk; others warn that facility strikes complicate verification and could increase proliferation risk if material is dispersed or hidden. — CSIS; IAEA; Mar 21
  • Sustainment: Debate persists over U.S. munitions stocks and budgetary sufficiency for a prolonged high‑tempo campaign; observers expect supplemental requests if operations continue. — CSIS; Brookings; Mar 11–20
  • Russian/Chinese assistance: Open‑source reporting raises questions about materiel or intelligence flows to Iran; assessments vary in confidence and require continued monitoring. — ISW; open reporting, Mar 14–21
CSIS Deep Dive

CSIS assessment through Mar 22 highlights sustained, high‑intensity strikes, growing concern about nuclear‑site operations, and widening missile reach that has increased regional risk and political pressure for posture adjustments.

  • Wide‑scale strikes and industrial degradation: Israeli forces continued wide‑scale airstrikes on Iranian defense‑industrial and missile production facilities, which outlets report have significantly degraded ballistic missile production capacity. — Reuters; The Jerusalem Post, Mar 21–22
  • Natanz strike and IAEA findings: An attack at the Natanz enrichment site prompted an IAEA investigation; the IAEA reported it was investigating and said it detected no rise in radiation levels outside the facility. — IAEA; Washington Post; Mar 21–22
  • Expanded missile reach and maritime threat: Iran launched ballistic missiles that officials said were aimed at Diego Garcia (reported not to have hit the base) and struck towns near Israel's nuclear site, demonstrating an expanded strike envelope and raising regional alarm. — Reuters; The Times of Israel; Al Jazeera, Mar 21–22
  • Political signaling on operations: U.S. political leaders signalled both continued deployments and consideration of a partial drawdown even as threats to energy infrastructure prompt threats of harsher measures if the Strait of Hormuz is not reopened. — Al Jazeera; The Times of Israel, Mar 21–22

Source: CSIS synthesis; IAEA; Reuters; Washington Post; The Jerusalem Post; Al Jazeera — Mar 22, 2026

ISW Deep Dive

ISW‑CTP reporting through Mar 22 documents continued strikes on Iranian military and defense‑industrial infrastructure, growing nuclear verification complications, and an expanded missile threat that reached distant targets.

  • Strikes on defense‑industrial sites: Reporting indicates targeted strikes on missile production and R&D facilities across Iran, consistent with efforts to degrade long‑range strike capabilities. — Reuters; The Jerusalem Post, Mar 21–22
  • Natanz incident and radiation checks: The Natanz enrichment facility was struck and the IAEA opened an investigation; the agency reported no detected increase in radiation levels outside the site. — IAEA; Washington Post; Mar 21–22
  • Missile impacts in Israel and broader reach: Iranian ballistic strikes caused injuries and damage near towns by Israel's Dimona/Arad area, underscoring a widening reach into Israeli territory. — Al Jazeera; The Jerusalem Post, Mar 21–22

Succession & Governance

Carnegie CFR RAND Brookings

Mojtaba Khamenei was selected as supreme leader on Mar 8 after his father was killed in the Feb 28 opening strikes. He has made few or no public appearances since selection, fueling questions about his health and the balance of power; U.S. and allied assessments indicate the IRGC is exercising outsized influence over governance and military decisions. — Carnegie; Brookings; The Jerusalem Post, Mar 8–21

Where they agree
  • Dynastic succession contradicts revolutionary principles but was the only viable option — Khamenei had pre‑stacked key institutions with loyalists; wartime conditions limited alternatives. — Carnegie; Brookings, Mar 6–11
  • No large‑scale security defections reported — security forces remain largely intact and loyal; elite defections would be the decisive indicator for regime fracture. — Brookings; Carnegie, Mar 11–21
  • If war ends now, Iran likely survives in a weakened but repressive form — survival, not victory, is the low bar that might constitute a political win for Tehran. — Brookings; Carnegie, Mar 10–21
Where they differ
  • Post‑war governance: CFR and other analysts differ on whether a managed transition ("Islamic Republic 2.0") is feasible or whether prolonged kinetic pressure will harden military rule under the IRGC. — CFR; RAND; Mar 11–21
  • Proxy viability: RAND sees proxy financing and sustainment degrading quicker than Tehran can adapt; Carnegie argues endurance doctrine preserves proxy utility longer. — RAND; Carnegie, Mar 2026

RAND Scenario Status Check

  • Suppression + Succession — dominant trajectory with Mojtaba installed and IRGC managing governance.
  • Popular Uprising — January protests were crushed and large‑scale elite defections have not materialized; this scenario remains possible but low probability without a major security split.
  • Forward defense doctrine facing strain — proxy networks and strategic depth are under pressure from coalition strikes and financial constraints. — RAND, Mar 2026

Carnegie: The Endurance Strategy

  • "Iran does not need to win — it needs to survive" (Grajewski). Survival under intense pressure is a plausible outcome that shapes Tehran's choices. — Carnegie, Mar 11
  • China's posture is transactional — Beijing maintains ties but avoids overt military rescue; economic lifelines are limited in scope. — Carnegie (Feigenbaum), Mar 2–21
  • Most likely outcome: a weakened Islamic Republic capable of ruling a devastated society, perhaps with increased repression and IRGC dominance. — Carnegie; Brookings, Mar 11–21

Energy, Maritime & Regional Spillover

CSIS CFR RAND Atl. Council Brookings Carnegie JINSA

The Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed to most commercial traffic; the halt to tanker flows and insurance withdrawals have produced a severe energy shock. Brent and other benchmarks spiked after strikes on South Pars and Ras Laffan; the U.S. waiver on Iranian oil at sea is an emergency measure to relieve market pressure. — Reuters; CSIS; Atlantic Council, Mar 20–21

Where they agree
  • Economic leverage through geography — Iran's ability to disrupt Hormuz imposes outsized costs on global markets and forces urgent policy tradeoffs for consumer economies. — Atlantic Council; Brookings; Mar 12–21
  • Energy market interventions — the U.S. waiver on some Iranian oil was explicitly designed to steady markets in the near term; its longer‑term strategic consequences are contested. — Reuters; New York Times; Mar 20
  • Gulf states moving from hedging to containment — diplomatic expulsions and limited kinetic responses signal Gulf states are increasingly willing to take punitive measures against Iran. — Reuters; Mar 21
Where they differ
  • Houthi activation risk: Some analysts warn Houthis could open a second major transit choke point in the Red Sea; others note operational constraints and competing incentives that may keep them quiescent. — CSIS; Atlantic Council, Mar 16–21
  • Duration of closure: Predictions vary on how long insurers will refuse Gulf war‑risk cover and how quickly traffic can normalize even after a pause in hostilities. — Brookings; Reuters, Mar 20–21

Civilian Impact & Accountability

CSIS Brookings CFR Alma Carnegie

Humanitarian needs continue to escalate: displacement, food and medicine shortages, and infrastructure damage are acute across Iran, Lebanon, and parts of Iraq. Reports of executions and harsh crackdowns persist inside Iran. The risk of damage to nuclear facilities adds a distinct civilian‑protection and environmental hazard. — UNHCR; Washington Post; AP; Mar 21

Where they agree
  • Escalating humanitarian toll — displacement, shortages, and civilian casualties are mounting; independent accountability mechanisms remain essential. — UN; Brookings; Mar 10–21
  • Nuclear‑site strikes raise unique risks — any kinetic damage to enrichment facilities amplifies risks of contamination, operational failures, and verification blind spots. — IAEA; Washington Post; Mar 21
Where they differ
  • Operational constraints: Some policymakers argue civilian‑casualty scrutiny will constrain future strikes; others believe transparency and legal scrutiny will not meaningfully slow kinetic operations in the near term. — Brookings; CSIS; Mar 11–21

  • Sustained multi‑axis strikes: Coalition and Israeli reporting describe continued, coordinated strikes on Iranian missile production, R&D, and command infrastructure. — Reuters; The Jerusalem Post, Mar 21–22
  • IAEA verification posture: The IAEA is investigating reported strikes at Natanz and has reported no detected rise in radiation levels outside the facility, though kinetic operations complicate long‑term verification. — IAEA; Washington Post, Mar 21–22
  • Regional spillover and missile reach: Iran's ballistic launches toward Diego Garcia (reported not to have hit the base) and strikes causing injuries near Israel's Dimona/Arad area indicate a broadened theater with higher escalation risk. — Reuters; Al Jazeera; The Times of Israel, Mar 21–22
  • Political and market pressure: U.S. leaders signalled both continued military posture and consideration of winding down operations even as threats to shipping and energy infrastructure keep markets and regional actors under strain. — Al Jazeera; Reuters, Mar 21–22
