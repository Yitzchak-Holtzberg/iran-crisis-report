Five Paths from Here

Scenarios are conditional pathways, not predictions with invented precision
How to use this page

Each scenario has a mechanism, observable indicators, and failure conditions. The ordinal labels describe present analytical weight, not mathematical probability: central means the path is already visible in current behavior; plausible means the mechanism exists but requires additional events; contingent means a specific break in current conditions is needed.

S1

Armed Pause and Declared Sufficiency

Central

Washington and Tehran each claim essential objectives, reduce sustained attacks, and return to an unstable version of the June framework. The United States points to degraded capability and protected navigation; Iran points to regime survival, retained deterrence, and negotiated relief. Hard nuclear and regional questions are deferred.

Indicators

  • A monitored pause in strikes without a comprehensive published agreement
  • Partial blockade relief and escorted or conditionally routed traffic
  • Indirect talks continue while both sides preserve maximal public claims
  • Israel retains an explicit right to act against reconstitution

Failure conditions

  • A vessel attack or disputed launch triggers automatic retaliation
  • No incident channel can determine attribution or proportional response
  • Commercial traffic remains too abnormal to sustain political support
  • Hidden nuclear activity or a denied inspection produces a new deadline

Why it is central: it asks the least of the parties and resembles behavior already observed. Its weakness is durability, not plausibility.

S2

Sequenced Negotiated Settlement

Plausible

A new agreement treats the crisis as several linked bargains rather than one grand exchange. Immediate attacks stop first; navigation and blockade rules follow; IAEA access and material accounting begin under a protected technical process; sanctions relief is phased against verified steps; regional and missile questions move to a longer forum.

Indicators

  • A written sequencing mechanism and a joint incident channel
  • IAEA access to affected facilities, records, personnel, and material
  • Insurance and shipping volumes recover without coercive routing rules
  • Relief and restrictions are reversible but not instantaneous

Failure conditions

  • Front-loaded relief without enforceable Iranian performance
  • Demands for complete capitulation before talks begin
  • Israeli operations or Iranian partner attacks remain outside the framework
  • Verification is promised politically but blocked operationally

Why it remains plausible: reciprocal pain creates incentives, and mediators retain access. Why it is not central: July showed that unresolved sequencing can destroy a paper framework quickly.

S3

Managed Authoritarian Transition

Plausible

Senior Iranian institutions conclude that perpetual war threatens the system more than selective compromise. A narrower ruling coalition—likely centered on security and state institutions—accepts limits, inspections, and regional restraint in exchange for survival, reconstruction, and sanctions relief. The Islamic Republic changes leadership style and policy without becoming democratic.

Indicators

  • Public deference to a collective or institutional decision process
  • Personnel changes that empower negotiators and economic managers
  • Security institutions enforce restraint on maritime and partner-force attacks
  • Reconstruction and revenue become explicit regime priorities

Failure conditions

  • Hardliners can veto implementation without bearing its cost
  • No leader can credibly bind dispersed military networks
  • External demands threaten institutional survival rather than behavior
  • Public anger makes elite accommodation look like capitulation

Why it is plausible: the state remains intact and highly institutionalized. What it would not mean: democratic legitimacy or resolution of Iran's internal rights crisis.

S4

Democratic Transition

Contingent

A broad internal coalition converts regime vulnerability into an organized transition. Protest and civic networks, minority movements, credible national figures, and parts of the state accept a temporary authority, a security compact, and a constitutional process. External actors protect the process without selecting Iran's next government.

Indicators

  • Sustained nationwide coordination despite communications controls
  • Verified, repeated security defections to a common civilian framework
  • A Pahlavi–republican–minority bargain or an accepted neutral alternative
  • Detailed continuity plans for services, borders, prisons, and nuclear security

Failure conditions

  • Opposition figures compete for recognition without internal organization
  • Centralization and autonomy disputes remain unresolved
  • Foreign military sponsorship destroys domestic legitimacy
  • State collapse outruns administrative and security coordination

Why it is contingent: the social constituencies exist, but the required coalition and governing machinery are not publicly visible at national scale.

S5

Chaotic Fragmentation

Contingent, high impact

Leadership, payroll, command, and public administration fail in different places at different speeds. Armed institutions fragment, regional and ethnic security dilemmas intensify, and no accepted transition authority emerges. The urgent international priorities become civilian protection, basic services, borders, and nuclear custodianship.

Indicators

  • Multiple verified command splits rather than isolated desertions
  • Provincial authorities stop following or receiving central orders
  • Competing armed groups seize infrastructure, prisons, or border crossings
  • Custodians of nuclear sites and material lose a recognized chain of authority

Mitigating conditions

  • A rapid elite compact around a single interim authority
  • Advance territorial and minority-rights guarantees
  • Protected UN and IAEA access accepted by relevant Iranian institutions
  • External restraint against proxy competition inside Iran

Why it matters despite uncertainty: impact is extreme and preparation cannot wait for a precise probability. Why it is not the baseline: the state continues to administer territory and conduct coordinated military and diplomatic action.

The scenario that is missing from simplistic forecasts

These paths can occur in sequence. An armed pause may become a negotiated settlement; a managed transition may later open or close political space; a failed democratic transition may fragment; a temporary deal may return to war. The useful question is not “Which box wins?” but “Which indicators are moving, and what decisions make a safer path more or less reachable?”
