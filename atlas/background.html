How This Crisis Was Built

Four histories converged: U.S.–Iranian hostility, a failed nuclear settlement, regional deterrence by proxy, and a domestic legitimacy crisis
The war did not begin with one decision

The February 2026 attack was a discrete choice. The conditions that made it thinkable accumulated over decades: the 1953 coup and 1979 revolution; hostage-taking and sanctions; the Iran–Iraq War; U.S. military dominance in the Gulf; Iran's partner networks; the rise and collapse of the JCPOA; the post-October 2023 regional wars; nuclear opacity after the 2025 strikes; and severe repression inside Iran.

01

U.S.–Iran Relations

A cycle of intervention, revolution, coercion, and failed accommodation
1953
The CIA and MI6 help overthrow Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh after the nationalization of oil. The event becomes a durable symbol of foreign intervention in Iranian political memory.
1979–1981
The revolution removes the Shah and creates the Islamic Republic. The U.S. Embassy hostage crisis transforms mutual suspicion into a foundational political relationship.
1980s
The Iran–Iraq War, U.S. support for Iraq, attacks on Gulf shipping, and direct U.S.–Iranian naval clashes shape Tehran's emphasis on missiles, irregular warfare, and strategic depth.
2001–2014
Limited tactical overlap in Afghanistan gives way to renewed hostility, sanctions, covert action, regional competition, and escalating nuclear concern.
2015
The JCPOA trades verifiable nuclear limits for sanctions relief. It narrows the nuclear risk without resolving missiles, regional forces, or the wider U.S.–Iran conflict.
2018–2024
The U.S. leaves the agreement and restores “maximum pressure.” Iran expands enrichment and regional coercion; both sides use calibrated attacks while avoiding full war.
2025–FEBRUARY 2026
Strikes, reduced inspection access, weakened partner networks, a U.S. force buildup, failed last-minute negotiations, and Iranian domestic repression create an unstable prewar environment.
02

The Nuclear File

A verification problem became a coercion problem, then a war aim
2002–2013
Previously undeclared facilities, safeguards disputes, enrichment growth, sanctions, and sabotage turn the program into an international crisis.
2015–2018
The JCPOA caps enrichment, reduces stockpiles, modifies facilities, and expands monitoring in exchange for relief.
2019–2024
After the U.S. withdrawal, Iran exceeds the agreement's limits and progressively reduces transparency. Efforts to restore the bargain fail.
2025 STRIKES
U.S. and Israeli attacks damage declared nuclear facilities. The strikes do not produce a verified accounting of enriched material or end the technical and political dispute.
2026 WAR
Further physical damage occurs while inspection continuity deteriorates. The central present problem is incomplete knowledge: facilities, material, activity, expertise, intent, and weaponization cannot be collapsed into one claim.
03

The Domestic Legitimacy Crisis

The war arrived after years of protest and an exceptionally violent prewar crackdown

Long roots

The 2009 Green Movement, economic protests in 2017–18 and 2019, and the 2022 Woman, Life, Freedom uprising broadened opposition to coercive rule. Labor, student, women's, minority, and professional networks developed overlapping but decentralized forms of resistance.

Late 2025–January 2026

A renewed nationwide protest wave met mass arrests, lethal force, and a sweeping communications shutdown. Casualty claims vary widely and remain difficult to verify. What is clear is that the state entered the war with a severe unresolved legitimacy problem and a strengthened coercive apparatus.

The Path to Operation Epic Fury

Deterrence failed because each side believed time and pressure favored action
PressureU.S.–Israeli readingIranian reading
Nuclear uncertaintyDelay increased the risk of a harder, less visible programRetained capability was leverage and insurance against attack
Regional balanceIran's weakened partners created a temporary operational opportunityMissiles, drones, and Hormuz could still deter or punish a campaign
NegotiationsThreat of force was required to compel acceptable limitsConcessions under threat would invite more demands
Domestic politicsIranian vulnerability could reduce resistance or accelerate changeExternal attack could consolidate the state and discredit opponents

CENTCOM says Operation Epic Fury began at 01:15 on 28 February 2026. The opening strikes targeted leadership, air defense, missile, drone, naval, security, and supporting infrastructure. Ali Khamenei was killed. Iran retaliated across the region and at sea. The campaign achieved major operational effects, but it did not settle the nuclear, maritime, regional, or political questions that produced it.

Sources include the CENTCOM opening-campaign fact sheet, IAEA safeguards history, and institutional analysis from IISS, RAND, and CSIS. This page uses history to explain causal structure, not to assign a single origin or moral equivalence.
