IR

Iran: Survival, Deterrence, and Bargaining Leverage

Tehran presents endurance as victory while using retaliation to preserve a place in the eventual settlement
The governing message

Iranian leaders frame the war as proof that military punishment cannot force surrender. Their public position joins four demands: an end to strikes, relief from blockade and sanctions pressure, recognition of an Iranian security role in Hormuz, and preservation of sovereign nuclear rights. The ambiguity is deliberate: it leaves room for mediated compromise while presenting concessions as resistance rather than capitulation.

What Tehran needs to demonstrate

  • The state and chain of command continue to function.
  • Retaliatory systems survive despite repeated strikes.
  • Commercial normalization requires Iranian consent or restraint.
  • Negotiators cannot trade away the revolution's core deterrent claims without compensation.

What constrains the posture

  • Attacks that alienate Gulf neighbors and strengthen the coalition against Iran.
  • Damage to infrastructure, public welfare, and export capacity.
  • The risk that continuing escalation exposes more replaceable and irreplaceable assets.
  • Internal disagreement over the acceptable price of survival.
Domestic legitimacy is not observable through state messaging alone

Funerals, rallies, military communiqués, and declarations of unity show that the state can mobilize and communicate. They do not establish representative public support. Civilian casualties, repression, economic loss, and interrupted services create powerful countervailing experiences that cannot be measured reliably under restricted access.

This section separates Iranian official positions from assessments of their effectiveness. See Carnegie on adaptation and RUSI on the longer test of resilience.

Gulf States: Security Without Entrapment

The states most exposed to Iranian retaliation also have the strongest interest in preventing an unlimited war
Anger runs in two directions

Gulf governments condemn attacks on their territory and depend on U.S. defenses. They also resent being exposed to a campaign whose objectives and duration they do not control. Their preferred outcome is not simply Iranian defeat: it is a stable order in which their cities, infrastructure, exports, and development strategies are no longer bargaining targets.

The shipping priority

Gulf exporters need more than escorted passage. They need predictable loading, routing, insurance, crewing, port operations, and legal conditions. June's partial recovery showed what diplomacy can unlock; July's renewed attacks showed how quickly confidence can reverse.

The political priority

Gulf states want U.S. protection to remain credible without converting every base or logistics relationship into automatic participation in an open-ended offensive campaign.

IL

Israel: Degradation Is Only Temporary Unless Reconstitution Is Stopped

Israel measures success against the future threat Iran could rebuild, not only the damage visible today
The strategic divergence with Washington

U.S. and Israeli interests overlap on preventing an Iranian nuclear weapon and reducing attacks. They diverge over the threshold for ending military operations. Washington must balance the campaign against force protection, Gulf stability, global economic effects, and war termination. Israel places greater weight on preventing reconstitution and preserving freedom to strike again.

Israeli theory of the case

  • Physical damage buys time only if procurement, repair, expertise, and political will are also constrained.
  • An inspection agreement that leaves hidden or inaccessible facilities unresolved is insufficient.
  • Iran's partner networks and missile production remain part of the same strategic problem.

Israeli constraints

  • Persistent missile and drone attacks impose civilian, military, and economic costs.
  • Long campaigns depend on U.S. logistics, munitions, diplomacy, and regional access.
  • Expanding target sets can increase international isolation and complicate an off-ramp.
Home-front performance is part of strategy

Layered defenses reduce harm but cannot make sustained attack costless. Interceptor use, disrupted aviation, work and school interruption, sheltering, damaged infrastructure, and public confidence shape how long Israel can sustain a campaign and how leaders define acceptable risk.

Sources: Atlantic Council on diverging war-termination interests; CSIS on the reconstitution problem.

The Wider World: Containing the Spillover

Energy, trade, food security, alliance credibility, and international law turn a regional war into a global problem
Most governments do not share one diagnosis, but they share an interest in limits

European and Asian importers, major trading states, and international organizations differ over responsibility and acceptable pressure on Iran. They broadly prefer restored navigation, protected civilian infrastructure, renewed inspections, and a credible process for ending repeated retaliation.

Import dependence, available inventories, fuel subsidies, refining configuration, and currency strength determine who bears the shock. Fertilizer, freight, and food costs transmit it far beyond the states purchasing Gulf crude directly.

Mediators

Pakistan, Oman, and Qatar provide access and face-saving channels, but cannot enforce compliance by themselves.

China and Russia

Both can influence Tehran and contest U.S. policy. Neither has yet taken ownership of Gulf security or commercial normalization.

International institutions

The IAEA, UN agencies, maritime bodies, and energy organizations provide verification and common data where political trust is weakest.

Global leverage is fragmented

No outside coalition presently combines the military access, economic incentives, inspection authority, regional legitimacy, and enforcement capacity needed for a comprehensive settlement. The likely diplomatic path is therefore modular: stabilize incidents first, restore verifiable access and commerce next, and negotiate harder political questions over a longer horizon.

Sources: IEA July Oil Market Report; EIA; Carnegie on wider regional effects.

Expert consensus

Gulf states want defense without permanent entrapment.

Their exposure to projectiles and shipping disruption makes U.S. protection important. The same exposure raises the cost of open-ended escalation and gives Gulf governments strong incentives to preserve mediation channels.

Carnegie IISS

Where they disagree

What security architecture should follow the war.

Positions diverge over deeper U.S. integration, regional self-reliance, relations with China, and whether accommodation with Iran is a stabilizing necessity or a source of renewed risk.
