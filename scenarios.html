Scenario 1

Regime Collapse — Revolution From Within

Likelihood: 30% — Most Likely Post-Strike Outcome
🔴 Day 8–9 Inflection: IRGC Fractures Watch; Succession Resolved but Contested

Core fracture indicators persist. Reports of a 17th Qods Corps (Sistan‑Baluchestan) stand‑down in select garrisons continue to circulate (unconfirmed; tier‑4/5 mix). Bank Melli payroll disruptions for IRGC‑linked entities have injected acute loyalty risk as the rial weakens. The Assembly of Experts named Mojtaba Khamenei Supreme Leader on Mar 3 — resolving the vacancy but activating a legitimacy crisis given his Hojjat ol‑Islam rank. External escalation (Bahrain strike; 7th U.S. death) rallies some loyalists but does not repair paychain or provincial control. — Sources: Reuters, Mar 3–4; AP, Mar 8; The Hill, Mar 9; Wall Street Journal (payroll), Mar 3; Janes (regional barrages), Mar 3

The Islamic Republic runs on an “avalanche model” of loyalty: cohesion appears monolithic until a visible defection survives retaliation. A first senior defection that endures would unlock rapid cascade. Khamenei’s death, sustained strikes, expanding protests, and a currency shock are converging. Day‑9 spillover buys time via rally‑around‑the‑flag effects, yet logistics and finances — the levers of loyalty — continue to erode.

  • First general‑rank defection — the catalytic signal flipping provincial command calculus.
  • Arafi’s stance — explicit endorsement vs. silence vs. dissent on Mojtaba will shape clerical cohesion.

The Transition Problem

  • No unified opposition command inside Iran — unmanaged collapse risks a Libya‑style vacuum.
  • IRGC economic capture (60–70%) — dismantling the Guards’ economic empire endangers livelihoods alongside power structures.
  • Nuclear material security gap — HEU and 20–60% stockpiles must be secured rapidly in a fluid environment. — Sources: IAEA/CFR, Mar 8
  • The Mojtaba wildcard — armed loyalists in Tehran/Qom could prolong violence even amid collapse. — Source: BBC, Mar 3
  • Historical precedent — Iraq 2003 and Libya 2011 warn against unmanaged institutional collapse.

Pahlavi Returns — Democratic Transition

Likelihood: 8% — Requires ordered collapse, not chaotic one
Day 8–9: Visibility Grows; Diplomatic Scaffolding Still Tentative

Reza Pahlavi’s “Prosperity Project” remains the most articulated post‑regime framework in circulation. International media attention and U.S. political signaling continue; White House language has edged toward “the Iranian people deserve a choice,” and back‑channels with advisers persist. Omani‑facilitated technical talks in Vienna remain tentative, preserving a narrow verification track that could intersect with a transition if collapse accelerates. — Sources: POLITICO, Mar 3; Washington Post, Mar 2; CBS News, Mar 2; Ynetnews (tier‑4 framing), Mar 4

⚠ Kurdish Fracture Risk: PJAK Still Not On Board

PJAK has not publicly endorsed the Prosperity Project, preserving a spoiler risk in the northwest absent autonomy guarantees. A senior Komala figure also linked any ground push to a U.S.-enforced no‑fly zone, underscoring preconditions that may be hard to meet quickly. Parallel reporting indicates thousands of Kurdish fighters in northern Iraq are preparing for potential cross‑border operations. — Sources: BBC, Feb 27; POLITICO, Mar 8; AP, Mar 3

Sc2 succeeds only if Sc1’s collapse is coordinated: multiple IRGC commanders pivot together, an interim authority secures clerical neutrality, and external partners underwrite immediate stabilization and nuclear security. Day‑9 spillover marginally aids junta rallying but also strains IRGC logistics and pay, potentially increasing incentives for a coordinated pivot if credible amnesty and security guarantees exist. — Sources: Reuters, Mar 3–4; CFR, Mar 8

  • Kurdish–Pahlavi gap — NFZ precondition and PJAK non‑endorsement persist. — Sources: POLITICO, Mar 8; BBC, Feb 27
  • Nuclear leverage — any IRGC faction controlling enriched uranium can bargain hard; requires rapid IAEA/US security ops. — Source: CFR, Mar 8

Likelihood: 7% — Under pressure as fractures and legitimacy issues mount

Israel’s claimed strike on the IRGC space/satellite HQ in Tehran would, if confirmed, further degrade the junta’s C2 and ISR ecosystem. Meanwhile, Gulf spillover (Bahrain desalination plant hit) underscores the externalization strategy to rally security organs under fire. — Sources: Reuters, AP, Mar 3–9; AP (Bahrain), Mar 8; Ynetnews, Mar 8 — not yet wire‑confirmed

  • Hossein Salami (IRGC Cdr) — coercive enforcement; lacks clerical legitimacy. — Source: Reuters, Mar 2
  • Esmail Qaani (Quds Force) — preserves proxy leverage (Houthis/PMF) amidst domestic strain. — Source: ISW, Mar 2
  • Ahmad Jannati (Guardian Council) — constitutional cover for Assembly outcomes. — Source: Reuters, Mar 2

Day‑9 posture: Mojtaba’s designation stands; Pezeshkian’s council claims continuity; Salami enforces internally; Qaani projects external pressure. International recognition absent; Western capitals signal non‑acceptance. — Sources: Reuters, AP, Mar 3–9

Sc3 is Sc1’s mirror: the same shocks that could topple the regime could be harnessed for coercive consolidation. But pay, provincial control, and clerical consent remain binding constraints. Without rapid alignment across IRGC fiefdoms and clerics, junta stability decays as the loyalty avalanche accelerates. — Sources: Reuters, Mar 3–4; CFR, Mar 8

Scenario 4

Iranian Civil War — State Fragmentation

Likelihood: 10% — Chaotic collapse variant; rises if Sc1 goes ungoverned
⚠ The Chaos Fork Widening: Independent Kurdish Moves Would Tip the Balance

Sc4 is Sc1 without coordination. New statements tying any Kurdish ground push to a U.S.-enforced no‑fly zone and reporting of operational preparations in northern Iraq increase the risk that regional armed actors seize vacated space before an interim authority coheres. Simultaneously, external barrages (e.g., Bahrain strike) divert IRGC resources, increasing the chance of localized vacuums. — Sources: POLITICO, Mar 8; AP, Mar 3; AP, Mar 8

Sc4 activates when collapse outpaces political organization: IRGC corps do not pivot in unison, ethnic militias assert local control, and Mojtaba‑aligned loyalists wage urban insurgency in Tehran/Qom. Nuclear material custodianship becomes contested across multiple actors — a proliferation worst case. — Sources: Foreign Affairs, Mar 6–8; CFR, Mar 8

Fragmentation Flashpoints

  • Kurdistan / Northwest (PJAK & allied factions) — no public endorsement of the Prosperity Project; NFZ precondition voiced; operational prep reported. — Sources: BBC, Feb 27; POLITICO, Mar 8; AP, Mar 3
  • Sistan‑Baluchestan (Jaish al‑Adl) — reported garrison irregularities indicate thinning IRGC control. — Source: Reuters, Mar 4
  • Khuzestan (Arab Ahvazi groups) — oil infrastructure becomes a prize amid Hormuz disruption. — Source: Reuters, Mar 2
  • Mojtaba loyalist redoubt — Basij/IRGC‑Intel cadres can sustain urban insurgency even post‑collapse. — Source: BBC, Mar 3

The Nuclear Proliferation Worst Case

  • Dispersed stockpiles — IAEA access unresolved; HEU/LEU at multiple sites risks multi‑actor competition. — Sources: IAEA statements; CFR, Mar 8
  • Secure‑and‑remove window shrinks — Pentagon/IAEA planning assumes an interlocutor; fragmentation removes it. — Source: Foreign Affairs, Mar 6–8
  • External accelerants — Russia/China hedge across factions; Gulf states may back Ahvazi groups; Turkey eyes Kurdish moves. — Sources: Foreign Affairs, Mar 6–8; CFR, Mar 8
  • Humanitarian shock — a 90M‑person state fragmenting under sanctions, war, and supply collapse produces outsized civilian harm. — Source: WHO/Guardian summaries, Mar 5 (context)
What Prevents Fragmentation

Prevention requires: (1) coordinated IRGC stand‑down to a single transitional authority; (2) binding autonomy guarantees for Kurdish/Baloch/Ahvazi groups before collapse; (3) rapid U.S./IAEA nuclear security within 48 hours; (4) Mojtaba neutralization/isolation to preclude a loyalist insurgency. — Sources: ISW, Carnegie, Reuters, Mar 3–9
