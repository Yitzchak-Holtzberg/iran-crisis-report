Scenario 1

Regime Collapse — Revolution From Within

Likelihood: 20% — Most Likely Post-Strike Outcome
🔴 Day 13–15 Inflection: IRGC Fractures Watch; Succession Legitimacy Under Strain

Core fracture indicators persist. Reports that elements of the 17th Qods Corps (Sistan‑Baluchestan) have limited operations continue to circulate (unconfirmed; tier‑4/5 mix). Payroll disruptions affecting IRGC‑linked entities and continuing currency stress are increasing acute loyalty risks. The Assembly of Experts named Mojtaba Khamenei Supreme Leader (Mar 3), resolving the vacancy but activating a legitimacy crisis. The recent strikes, including the Kharg Island attack, raise the stakes for internal cohesion by directly threatening economic and logistical lifelines. — Sources: Reuters, Mar 3–13; AP, Mar 12; AP/CNN, Mar 14

The Islamic Republic runs on an “avalanche model” of loyalty: cohesion appears monolithic until a visible defection survives retaliation. A first senior defection that endures would unlock rapid cascade. Mojtaba’s contested legitimacy, sustained external strikes, expanding protests, and a currency shock are converging. Recent external pressure on oil chokepoints (Kharg/ Hormuz) both rallies some loyalists and further strains the regime’s logistical and financial capacity.

  • 17th Qods Corps (Sistan‑Baluchestan) operational irregularities — REPORTED (unconfirmed). Multiple town‑level stand‑down allegations persist; no wire‑confirmed full corps order. — Source: Reuters, Mar 4 (unconfirmed)
  • Bank Melli IRGC subsidiary payroll failure — TRIGGERED (one cycle). Next payroll window imminent; a repeat would be loyalty‑breaking. — Source: WSJ, Mar 3
  • Mojtaba Khamenei succession — RESOLVED → LEGITIMACY CRISIS ACTIVE. Rank below expectations; watch Ayatollah Alireza Arafi for clerical alignment/split. — Source: Reuters, AP, Mar 3
  • CIA defection channel first contact — REPORTED (tier‑5/4 mix; requires corroboration). No general‑rank confirmation. — Source: open‑source summaries, Mar 3
  • Externalization via proxies & maritime pressure — ACTIVE. Mass barrages at Gulf states and confirmed strikes on tanker routes raise economic and cohesion pressure. — Sources: Janes, AP, Reuters, Mar 3–14
  • Nuclear material security — WATCHING. IAEA access unresolved; secure‑and‑remove planning by Western militaries remains active as a contingency. — Sources: IAEA statements; CFR, Mar 9; Reuters/Bloomberg, Mar 8–12

Remaining Signals to Watch

  • First general‑rank defection — the catalytic signal flipping provincial command calculus.
  • Arafi’s stance — explicit endorsement vs. silence vs. dissent on Mojtaba will shape clerical cohesion.
  • Repeat payroll failure — a second missed cycle would likely trigger unit‑level stand‑downs. — Source: WSJ, Mar 3
  • Additional corps stand‑downs — confirmation beyond Sistan‑Baluchestan would validate the avalanche model. — Source: Reuters, Mar 4
  • Nuclear site access — IAEA on‑site verification of Natanz/Isfahan centrifuge status remains decisive. — Sources: IAEA statements; CFR, Mar 9

The Transition Problem

  • No unified opposition command inside Iran — unmanaged collapse risks a Libya‑style vacuum.
  • IRGC economic capture (60–70%) — dismantling the Guards’ economic empire endangers livelihoods alongside power structures.
  • Nuclear material security gap — HEU and mid‑/high‑enriched LEU stockpiles must be secured rapidly in a fluid environment. — Sources: IAEA/CFR, Mar 9
  • The Mojtaba wildcard — armed loyalists in Tehran/Qom could prolong violence even amid collapse. — Source: BBC, Mar 3
  • Humanitarian and refugee flows — intensified strikes and fragmentation would produce major displacement across borders. — Source: WHO/UN reporting context, Mar 5–14
Scenario 2

Pahlavi Returns — Democratic Transition

Likelihood: 6% — Requires ordered collapse, not chaotic one
Day 13–15: Visibility Grows; Diplomatic Scaffolding Still Tentative

Reza Pahlavi’s “Prosperity Project” remains the most articulated post‑regime framework in circulation. International attention and back‑channel diplomacy persist; reports indicate regional intermediaries (Oman, Turkey, Egypt) are pushing for talks that could create technical scaffolding for a transition if collapse is orderly. These tracks are still fragile and contingent on IRGC pivot and immediate stabilization after recent strikes. — Sources: POLITICO, Mar 3; Washington Post, Mar 2; Ynetnews, Mar 4

⚠ Kurdish Fracture Risk: PJAK Still Not On Board

PJAK and other Kurdish factions have not publicly endorsed the Prosperity Project, preserving a spoiler risk in the northwest absent autonomy guarantees. Recent reporting of operational preparations among some Kurdish fighters in northern Iraq could complicate a smooth, centralized transition. — Sources: BBC, Feb 27; POLITICO, Mar 8; AP, Mar 3

Sc2 succeeds only if Sc1’s collapse is coordinated: multiple IRGC commanders pivot together, an interim authority secures clerical neutrality, and external partners underwrite immediate stabilization and nuclear security. Recent diplomatic activity increases theoretical feasibility of a managed transition but remains tenuous amid continued strikes, Kharg Island disruption, and regional instability. — Sources: Reuters, Mar 3–14; CFR, Mar 9

What Would Trigger This

  • Coordinated IRGC senior defections — 3–5 generals jointly back a transitional authority.
  • Khamenei death — CONFIRMED (Mar 1) — authority vacuum leveraged for transition architecture. — Source: AP, Mar 1
  • U.S. recognition signal — back‑channel becomes formal endorsement. — Source: POLITICO, Mar 3
  • Vienna technical track + IAEA access — verification pathway aligned to transition milestones. — Sources: CBS News, Mar 2; IAEA statements
  • Kurdish alignment — PJAK/Komala/SDF endorse a unified framework to avoid a northwest insurgency. — Source: BBC, Feb 27; POLITICO, Mar 8

Critical Obstacles

  • Mojtaba’s network — IRGC Intelligence and Basij ideological units can spoil absent neutralization or co‑optation. — Source: Reuters, Mar 3
  • Kurdish–Pahlavi gap — NFZ precondition and PJAK non‑endorsement persist. — Sources: POLITICO, Mar 8; BBC, Feb 27
  • Nuclear leverage — any IRGC faction controlling enriched uranium can bargain hard; requires rapid IAEA/US security ops. — Source: CFR, Mar 9
  • Institutional vacuum — no party machine or bureaucracy ready to govern; reliance on regime technocrats risks legitimacy.
Historical Parallel — Five Simultaneous Shifts Still Holding

Recent days still reflect February’s shift set: (1) IRGC finances under acute pressure; (2) the opposition has a focal leader; (3) a defection channel reportedly exists; (4) U.S. signaling more permissive toward recognition; (5) Khamenei’s death removed the ultimate authority figure. The open question is conversion into a coordinated move before Sc1 turns chaotic or Sc3 re‑coheres around Mojtaba. — Sources: CBS/60 Minutes, Mar 3; POLITICO, Mar 3; Reuters, Mar 3

Scenario 3

IRGC Junta — Succession Council Consolidates Power

Likelihood: 6% — Under pressure as fractures and legitimacy issues mount
Day 12–15: External Escalation Continues; IRGC C2 Under Fire

Official and open‑source reporting shows continued strikes against IRGC infrastructure inside Iran and a widening of the battlefield into Lebanon and maritime lanes. The Assembly of Experts’ naming of Mojtaba Khamenei (Mar 3) remains the junta’s core legitimacy play — and its Achilles’ heel. If Western strikes further degrade IRGC command and control, the junta’s ability to project control could weaken. Recent targeting of Kharg Island increases operational strain on logistics and economic levers that the IRGC relies upon. — Sources: Reuters, AP, Mar 3–14

🔴 Who Would Actually Run the Junta

Constitutionally, the First VP, parliament speaker, and judiciary chief manage the interim. Practically, four figures hold the levers:

  • Mojtaba Khamenei — named Supreme Leader (Mar 3); controls IRGC intelligence/Basij networks; legitimacy contested. Watch Ayatollah Alireza Arafi for clerical acceptance. — Sources: Reuters, AP, Mar 3
  • Hossein Salami (IRGC Cdr) — coercive enforcement; lacks clerical legitimacy. — Source: Reuters, Mar 2
  • Esmail Qaani (Quds Force) — preserves proxy leverage (Houthis/PMF/Hezbollah) amidst domestic strain. — Source: ISW, Mar 2
  • Ahmad Jannati (Guardian Council) — constitutional cover for Assembly outcomes. — Source: Reuters, Mar 2

Day‑15 posture: Mojtaba’s designation stands; Pezeshkian’s council claims continuity; Salami enforces internally; Qaani projects external pressure. The junta retains coercive tools but faces growing operational friction as strikes target infrastructure and as maritime disruption robs the state of export revenues. International recognition remains absent; Western capitals signal non‑acceptance. — Sources: Reuters, AP, Mar 3–14

Sc3 is Sc1’s mirror: the same shocks that could topple the regime could be harnessed for coercive consolidation. But pay, provincial control, clerical consent, and intact C2 remain binding constraints. Without rapid alignment across IRGC fiefdoms and clerics, junta stability decays as the loyalty avalanche accelerates.

The Mojtaba Factor — Day 12–15 Update

  • Designation confirmed by the Assembly on Mar 3; rank‑based legitimacy gap persists; clerical signaling (Arafi) decisive. — Sources: Reuters, AP, Mar 3
  • Immediate‑term cohesion signal — independent assessment indicates the appointment likely sustains elite cohesion in the near term unless pay/logistics collapse. — Source: Janes, Mar 9
  • Russia/China likely prefer continuity and will signal quietly, but are unlikely to back a losing hand publicly. — Source: Foreign Affairs/CFR analyses, Mar 6–12

Why the Junta Is Struggling

  • IRGC fiefdoms — Aerospace, Ground, Navy, Quds, Intelligence, Basij must align; wartime pressures magnify competition.
  • Provincial disruption — expanding unrest and reported garrison irregularities undercut projection of control. — Source: Reuters, Mar 4
  • Economic freefall — oil export disruption and Hormuz standstill worsen material support to loyal units. — Sources: CNBC, Bloomberg, Mar 9–13
  • International rejection — Western non‑recognition and sanctions expansion inhibit consolidation. — Source: Foreign Affairs, Mar 6–12
If the Junta Somehow Consolidates: Consequences

Expect nationwide emergency law, detentions, conditional IAEA access, proxy activation to deter intervention, and elevated nuclear weaponization risk under existential‑threat logic. Recent strikes on export hubs increase pressure to securitize nuclear and energy assets. — Sources: IAEA statements; ISW, Mar 2; RAND/CFR analyses, Mar 6–14

Scenario 4

Iranian Civil War — State Fragmentation

Likelihood: 6% — Chaotic collapse variant; rises if Sc1 goes ungoverned
⚠ The Chaos Fork Widening: Independent Kurdish Moves Would Tip the Balance

Sc4 is Sc1 without coordination. New statements tying any Kurdish ground push to a U.S.‑enforced no‑fly zone and reporting of operational preparations in northern Iraq increase the risk that regional armed actors seize vacated space before an interim authority coheres. The Kharg Island strike and continued maritime interdictions divert IRGC resources and shorten the window for orderly transition. — Sources: POLITICO, Mar 8; AP, Mar 3; BBC, Feb 27; Reuters, Mar 13–14

Sc4 activates when collapse outpaces political organization: IRGC corps do not pivot in unison, ethnic militias assert local control, and Mojtaba‑aligned loyalists wage urban insurgency in Tehran/Qom. Nuclear material custodianship becomes contested across multiple actors — a proliferation worst case.

Fragmentation Flashpoints

  • Kurdistan / Northwest (PJAK & allied factions) — no public endorsement of the Prosperity Project; NFZ precondition voiced; operational prep reported. — Sources: BBC, Feb 27; POLITICO, Mar 8
  • Sistan‑Baluchestan (Jaish al‑Adl) — reported garrison irregularities indicate thinning IRGC control. — Source: Reuters, Mar 4
  • Khuzestan (Arab Ahvazi groups) — oil infrastructure becomes a prize amid Hormuz disruption. — Source: Reuters, Mar 2
  • Mojtaba loyalist redoubt — Basij/IRGC‑Intel cadres can sustain urban insurgency even post‑collapse. — Source: BBC, Mar 3

The Nuclear Proliferation Worst Case

  • Dispersed stockpiles — IAEA access unresolved; HEU/LEU at multiple sites risks multi‑actor competition. — Sources: IAEA statements; CFR, Mar 9
  • Secure‑and‑remove window shrinks — Pentagon/IAEA planning assumes an interlocutor; fragmentation removes it. — Source: Foreign Affairs, Mar 6–12
  • External accelerants — Russia/China hedge across factions; Gulf states may back Ahvazi groups; Turkey eyes Kurdish moves. — Sources: Foreign Affairs, Mar 6–12; Reuters, Mar 13
  • Humanitarian shock — a 90M‑person state fragmenting under sanctions, war, and supply collapse produces outsized civilian harm. — Source: WHO/UN summaries, Mar 5–14 (context)
What Prevents Fragmentation

Prevention requires: (1) coordinated IRGC stand‑down to a single transitional authority; (2) binding autonomy guarantees for Kurdish/Baloch/Ahvazi groups before collapse; (3) rapid U.S./IAEA nuclear security within a narrow window; (4) Mojtaba neutralization/isolation to preclude a loyalist insurgency. Recent strikes on export nodes make these windows shorter. — Sources: ISW, Carnegie, Reuters, Mar 3–14

Scenario 5

Regional Escalation — Proxy War Spreads Beyond Iran

Likelihood: 62% — Rung 1 fully active; Rung 2 widening — multi‑front spillover observed
⚠ Day 13–15: Escalation Ladder Accelerates — Kharg Strike Raises Tensions

Rung 1 remains fully active across theaters (missile/drone harassment; Hezbollah re‑entry). Rung 2 widened as Hezbollah and Lebanese fronts saw renewed heavy exchanges and Iran continued strikes on shipping and Gulf infrastructure; the confirmed strike on Kharg Island — combined with tanker attacks and terminal strikes — intensifies the risk of broader Gulf state responses and raises the odds of further international military involvement. — Sources: The Guardian, Washington Post, AP, Reuters, Mar 12–14 (Tier 2–1)

Sc5 is the pathway where the U.S.–Iran strike exchange expands into a multi‑state war. As of Day 15, the conflict has spilled into multiple neighboring theatres — Lebanon (Hezbollah/IDF exchanges), Gulf shipping (tankers hit), and attacks on Gulf infrastructure — though GCC states have so far avoided formal co‑belligerency. U.S. deployments and amphibious assets increase the options for escort operations and potential escalation steps. Containment drivers remain: Turkey/NATO thresholds uninvoked, and U.S./Israeli SEAD/ISR continue to pressure proxy launch capacity. — Sources: AP, Bloomberg, Reuters, Mar 12–14

Escalation Ladder

  • Rung 1: Proxy/militia harassment + Gulf barrages — FULLY ACTIVE. Mass drone/missile attacks; Hezbollah re‑entry; PMF and Houthi activity resume. — Sources: AP, Janes, Mar 2–14
  • Rung 2: Infrastructure strikes + U.S./allied casualties — EXPANDING. Multiple incidents against tankers and Gulf terminals; civilian infrastructure damaged; confirmed allied fatalities and recent strikes on export infrastructure have increased political pressure on capitals. — Sources: The Hill, AP, Mar 8–14
  • Rung 2 economic signal: Force majeure — several energy firms declared force majeure as regional operations were disrupted. — Source: Euronews/market reporting, Mar 9–13 (Tier 4)
  • Rung 3: Gulf counter‑engagement — Saudi/UAE kinetic options remain possible with U.S. ISR/SEAD support; not yet fully activated but thresholds lowered by recent maritime attacks and the Kharg strike. — Source: CNBC, Mar 9–13
  • Rung 4: Iraq state fracture — PMF tensions and attacks on bases increase risk to U.S. basing and regional stability. — Source: WSJ/Reuters, Mar 8–13
  • Rung 5: Great‑power overlay — China and Russia press for de‑escalation publicly while hedging diplomatically; direct backing to Tehran remains constrained. — Sources: Foreign Affairs, Mar 6–12; Reuters, Mar 12–14

Strait of Hormuz — The Economic Trigger

  • Near standstill — commercial tanker traffic is sharply reduced; insurers and operators continue to avoid transit. — Sources: Bloomberg, Reuters, Mar 12–13
  • Record supply shock — market analyses and IEA commentary describe the current disruption as among the largest global oil shocks; prices remain highly volatile above $100 per barrel. — Sources: IEA/NBC, Bloomberg, World Oil, Mar 12–14
  • Alternative routes — Saudi/UAE pipeline rerouting and emergency strategic releases are easing some immediate shortfalls but do not replace full seaborne capacity. — Source: CNBC, Mar 12
  • Re‑opening triggers — coordinated de‑mining, security escorts, and insurer re‑engagement would be required to restore normal traffic; any confirmed use of mines or seizures would likely trigger a larger naval response. — Sources: CFR, NYT, Mar 9–14
Why This Scenario Is Still Contained — And Triggers to Watch

Sc5 remains at 62% because GCC states avoid formal co‑belligerency, Turkey/NATO thresholds remain uninvoked, and U.S./Israeli SEAD/ISR keep degrading proxy launch capacity. Triggers for a large jump: (a) a second mass‑casualty allied incident; (b) Gulf state formal entry into hostilities; (c) Hezbollah shift from episodic strikes to sustained campaign; (d) confirmed mining/seizure of Hormuz; (e) credible great‑power military backing to Tehran. The Kharg Island strike and increased U.S. amphibious deployments lower the margin for miscalculation. — Sources: ISW, AP, Janes, Reuters, Mar 9–14
