The Regional System

A war led by two strike powers, resisted through Iranian dispersion and geography, and absorbed by states that want protection without permanent belligerency
The balance is asymmetric, not ambiguous

The United States and Israel hold the conventional initiative. They can reach targets across Iran, sustain air and maritime operations, and force Iran to disperse. Iran cannot match that power symmetrically. It can still make the campaign costly by threatening exposed bases, cities, ports, energy infrastructure, and shipping. The resulting contest is therefore less about who controls the air than about whether military superiority can produce a durable political order.

Gulf states: protected and exposed

Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Oman depend on U.S. security support and need Iranian attacks stopped. They also bear the immediate cost of escalation: damage to airports and infrastructure, disrupted energy exports, insurance shocks, and the political risk of appearing to host a war against a neighbor.

  • Shared preference: restore predictable commerce and prevent attacks on their territory.
  • Shared anxiety: neither a dominant Iran nor an open-ended U.S.–Israeli campaign serves their long-term interests.
  • Different roles: Oman, Qatar, and Pakistan have emphasized mediation; other Gulf states are more deeply embedded in the defensive architecture.

Outside powers: influence without ownership

China and Russia benefit from limits on U.S. freedom of action and maintain ties to Tehran, but neither has supplied the security, commercial guarantees, or enforcement capacity required to settle the conflict. European governments have greater interest in inspection, energy stability, and legal restraint than operational control of the war.

  • China: energy access and regional stability, without assuming the Gulf security burden.
  • Russia: diplomatic and technical support for Iran, constrained by its other commitments.
  • Europe: inspection access, shipping normalization, and limits on escalation.

Air Power: Access Is Not the Same as Resolution

The United States and Israel can operate across Iran; the unresolved question is what repeated access can achieve against dispersed systems and political objectives
Current assessment

Iran's integrated air-defense network and conventional air force have been severely degraded. U.S. and Israeli aircraft have repeatedly struck fixed and mobile targets throughout the country. That operational freedom is real. It does not establish that every launcher, drone unit, command node, underground facility, or production chain has been destroyed—and it does not answer how the war ends.

What air dominance enables

  • Repeated attacks on command, air-defense, missile, drone, naval, logistics, and supporting infrastructure.
  • Persistent surveillance and rapid re-attack of emitters or units that expose themselves.
  • Protection of large naval and air formations through layered defensive and offensive operations.
  • Pressure on Iran's ability to concentrate, communicate, repair, and move openly.

What it does not guarantee

  • Complete knowledge of mobile launchers, stored munitions, underground assets, or decoys.
  • Permanent destruction of a repairable site or a replaceable component.
  • Defense of every exposed civilian or military target against low-cost salvos.
  • A political decision in Tehran to stop maritime attacks, accept inspections, or abandon reconstitution.

The air campaign as a cycle

StageU.S.–Israeli advantageIranian adaptation

The defense-cost problem

Iran does not need to penetrate every defense. It can use repeated, mixed-cost attacks to consume interceptors, force dispersal, interrupt airports and ports, and impose uncertainty on civilians and markets. RUSI's work on loitering munitions and CSIS's work on reconstitution both point to the same structural problem: inexpensive and replaceable threats can remain consequential after sophisticated fixed systems are destroyed.

Sources: CENTCOM fact sheet for the U.S. description of the opening campaign; CSIS on reconstitution; RUSI on loitering munitions. Target and damage claims by belligerents are not treated here as a complete independent loss ledger.

Maritime Power Beyond Fleet Counts

The United States controls the high end of the naval contest; Iran can still suppress commerce with coastal systems, small craft, mines, drones, and political uncertainty
A destroyed navy is not a normalized Strait

Public evidence supports heavy damage to Iranian naval facilities and vessels. It does not follow that commercial shipping is safe or ordinary. The tools most useful for disruption are dispersed across coastlines, islands, ports, civilian-looking craft, mobile launchers, mines, and command networks. RUSI and IISS both conclude that no plausible purely military operation can eliminate every threat or restore the confidence on which normal commerce depends.

Sea control

U.S. forces can concentrate firepower, escort selected vessels, strike coastal targets, and defeat conventional Iranian formations.

Sea denial

Iran can threaten routes, create intermittent danger, complicate identification, and force expensive defensive behavior without sealing the waterway.

Commercial confidence

Owners, insurers, crews, ports, and charterers make independent decisions. Physical passage can resume well before normal traffic does.

U.S. maritime posture

Carrier, surface, submarine, air, and land-based forces provide strike, escort, interception, surveillance, and blockade-enforcement capacity. Exact disposition changes quickly and should be read from current official releases, not treated as a standing order of battle.

ACTIVE THEATER FORCE

Reserve and rotation context

Named ships and air wings rotate, return to homeport, and re-enter readiness cycles. A carrier's location is useful current status, not a durable measure of total U.S. capacity.

ROTATIONAL CAPACITY

Best current reading

The United States can prevent Iran from exercising conventional naval command of the Strait. It cannot cheaply guarantee that every commercial transit is safe. Iran's leverage survives when a small number of attacks, credible threats, or administrative demands change the behavior of thousands of private decisions. That is why a political arrangement and reliable command compliance are part of maritime security, not an optional diplomatic afterthought.

Sources: UKMTO/JMIC for current maritime risk; IISS and RUSI on the limits of military normalization; IEA for the energy-flow context.
