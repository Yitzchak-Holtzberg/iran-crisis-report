Carnegie

Carnegie Endowment for International Peace

Carnegie's Karim Sadjadpour, the leading US analyst of Iranian domestic politics, frames the post-Khamenei succession as "the most dangerous transition in Middle Eastern politics since 1979." Carnegie's analysis prioritizes the Mojtaba Khamenei variable — describing him as a figure who was "systematically positioned to inherit his father's mantle" but who faces a legitimacy deficit that may prove fatal to the junta scenario.

The Succession Analysis (Sadjadpour)

  • Mojtaba's legitimacy problem: He is not a senior cleric and cannot claim the velayat-e faqih (guardianship of the jurist) that underpins the Islamic Republic's constitutional legitimacy; any attempt to rule would be transparently dynastic — the opposite of the 1979 revolution's founding ideology
  • Assembly of Experts: The constitutional body responsible for selecting a new Supreme Leader is 88 members, median age 72, and split between hardliners and pragmatists. Carnegie assesses Mojtaba can secure a slim majority but not the two-thirds needed for a politically legitimate selection
  • The reformist wildcard: Carnegie identifies a small bloc of reformist clerics inside the Assembly who may see the post-strike chaos as their last opportunity to assert influence; their alignment could determine whether the succession produces a junta or a compromise figure

Carnegie: Democratic Transition Conditions

  • Five simultaneous conditions now exist that have never previously been true: (1) IRGC economic base under destruction; (2) protest movement has a named leader for the first time in 46 years; (3) CIA operational defection channel is live; (4) Trump administration back-channel to Pahlavi confirmed; (5) Khamenei dead, removing the authority figure who could order unified resistance
  • The conversion problem: Carnegie warns that conditions ≠ outcome — Iran's opposition has had favorable conditions before (2009, 2019) and failed to convert them; the difference this time is the external military dimension, which changes the IRGC's internal calculation
  • Kurdish fracture as spoiler: Carnegie assesses the Pahlavi–Kurdish dissident split as the single most likely obstacle to a coordinated transition; PJAK in the northwest could open a low-grade insurgency within days of any transitional authority announcement
Brookings

Brookings Institution

The Day-After Problem

Brookings' Iran analysts draw the sharpest historical parallel: Iraq 2003. The US dismantled a state apparatus in 21 days and spent 20 years managing the consequences. Iran has four times Iraq's population, an intact nuclear program, and a protest movement that has named an external leader rather than emerged organically from within. Brookings flags three critical planning failures it assesses the US has not addressed: (1) no post-strike civil administration plan for 31 provinces with IRGC-dominated economies; (2) nuclear material chain-of-custody gap — 1,000+ kg of enriched uranium at dispersed sites with no US/IAEA pre-positioned rapid-response teams; (3) economic reconstitution financing — $100B+ in frozen assets cannot be unlocked without Congressional action and EU agreement that have not yet been initiated. Brookings does not assess that military strikes alone produce a stable outcome without these three elements.

Mar 2 Update: Brookings flags the Qatar LNG force majeure as a structural escalation in the economic dimension — the event crosses from market disruption into supply-chain emergency for US allies. Brookings calls for an immediate G7 energy emergency coordination mechanism to prevent European winter energy shortfalls from fracturing alliance cohesion on the Iran operation. — Source: Brookings, Mar 2, 2026; GCaptain/AP, Mar 2, 2026

CFR

Council on Foreign Relations — Global Consequences Assessment

🌎 The International Order Inflection Point (Mar 2, 2026)

CFR's Iran Working Group assesses that Operation Epic Fury has triggered the most significant redrawing of the post-1945 security architecture since the 2003 Iraq War — and potentially since 1991. The US has conducted unprovoked first-strikes on a sovereign state's nuclear program and confirmed the killing of a sitting Head of State without a UN Security Council authorization or coalition endorsement. CFR identifies five structural consequences that will outlast the immediate conflict: (1) NPT erosion: every non-nuclear state now faces a binary choice between rapid weaponization and US-guaranteed security — the treaty's deterrence value is permanently diminished; (2) UN Security Council paralysis: Russian and Chinese vetoes will block any post-conflict reconstruction authorization; (3) Alliance fracture: Germany, France, and Turkey have refused to endorse the operation; (4) Precedent for pre-emptive strikes: Israel, India, and potentially Pakistan will cite this operation as authorization for pre-emptive nuclear-related strikes against perceived threats; (5) Dollar-oil peg stress: Gulf producers may accelerate non-dollar oil settlement conversations with China. — Source: CFR Iran Working Group, Mar 2, 2026

CFR: Great Power Dynamics (Mar 2)

  • Russia's calculated ambiguity: Moscow has issued a "supportive statement" for Tehran (TIME, Mar 2) without committing material support — CFR assesses Russia is using the crisis to extract US concessions on Ukraine while avoiding direct confrontation; this limits Russian leverage as a spoiler but keeps Moscow as a necessary party in any diplomatic settlement
  • China's economic veto: China imports ~10% of its oil through the Strait of Hormuz; a prolonged closure triggers its own economic emergency. CFR assesses Beijing will privately pressure Tehran to accept the Oman framework to protect its energy supply chain — China is simultaneously Tehran's economic lifeline and its most effective moderating influence
  • NATO cohesion stress test: Germany and France's refusal to endorse the operation is the most significant transatlantic fracture since Iraq 2003. CFR warns that if US casualties mount, domestic pressure will force the administration to seek NATO endorsement retroactively — a request that will be denied, exposing alliance strains to public scrutiny
  • India's strategic calculation: India abstained from UN emergency session (as expected), but CFR notes India is the third-largest importer of Iranian oil via informal channels — New Delhi faces a secondary sanctions threat and may push for a back-channel humanitarian corridor to protect its economic exposure

CFR: Diplomatic Path Assessment (Mar 2)

  • Oman channel viability: CFR assesses the Oman "zero enrichment" agreement in principle is genuine but fragile — Oman's credibility as a mediator depends on its ability to deliver the IRGC leadership council, which is consolidating under uncertain command structure; the deal requires the council to accept terms before it has completed its own internal power negotiation
  • Sanctions architecture as leverage: The US has not yet removed any Iran sanctions — CFR identifies this as the key US bargaining chip; a credible commitment to sanctions relief within a verified framework could accelerate the Oman deal
  • IAEA verification challenge: Any deal requires IAEA inspectors on the ground within days of signing; Iran's remaining nuclear sites are damaged and some are potentially radiologically contaminated — CFR assesses IAEA capacity is insufficient for rapid deployment at this scale without emergency funding authorization
  • The 72-hour window: CFR concurs with CSIS and ISW — the Oman framework must advance within 72–96 hours or the consolidating leadership council will adopt a hardline stance that closes the deal window; CFR recommends a direct Secretary of State–led diplomatic mission to Muscat to accelerate the timeline
RAND

RAND Corporation — Economic & Systemic Risk Analysis

📈 The Economic Weapon: Iran's Hormuz Strategy and Global Systemic Risk (Mar 2, 2026)

RAND's National Security Research Division assesses that Iran's most powerful remaining asymmetric weapon is not military but economic: the ability to impose global systemic costs through Hormuz disruption far exceeding the costs Iran itself incurs. RAND models show that a sustained 30-day Hormuz closure drives WTI to $145–160 and Brent to $155–175, triggering recession conditions in the EU, Japan, and South Korea within 60 days. This is the "rational pain trade" from Iran's perspective: it cannot defeat the US militarily, but it can impose economic costs on US allies that fracture the coalition and accelerate political pressure for a ceasefire. RAND assesses the IRGC Navy's mining and fast-attack capability — fully intact as of Mar 2 — is the single most consequential unresolved military variable in the conflict. — Source: RAND NSRD, Mar 2026; AP/GCaptain, Mar 2, 2026

RAND: Economic Scenarios (Mar 2)

  • Scenario A — Deal within 5 days: Oman framework advances to a verified ceasefire; Hormuz reopens within 7–10 days; oil retraces to $95–105; ~150 stranded vessels clear; global recession risk recedes. RAND assigns 25% probability
  • Scenario B — Stalemate 2–4 weeks: IRGC Navy maintains partial Hormuz disruption without full closure; oil stays at $110–125; insurance withdrawals accelerate; EU energy emergency declarations begin by Week 3. RAND assigns 45% probability — the most likely economic outcome
  • Scenario C — Full Hormuz closure: IRGC mines the strait and dares the US Navy to de-mine under fire; WTI spikes to $145+; Qatar LNG disruption becomes persistent; global recession by Week 8. RAND assigns 30% probability, elevated from pre-strike baseline of 10%
  • Iran rial collapse: The rial is already trading near 1.7M/USD (Iran International, Mar 1) — RAND models that the currency reaches 2.5M/USD within 14 days of continued conflict, rendering IRGC soldier payroll effectively worthless and accelerating loyalty collapse among rank-and-file units

RAND: Systemic Risk Flags (Mar 2)

  • US homeland cyber exposure: DHS Mar 2 alert on targeted and cyber threats aligns with RAND's pre-war assessment that Iran's cyber units (APT33/Refined Kitten, APT35/Charming Kitten) would be activated for homeland infrastructure attacks as retaliation; RAND forecasts phishing and supply-chain compromise attacks on US financial infrastructure within 5–7 days if negotiations fail
  • Insurance market cascade: Insurers' withdrawal of tanker coverage (AP/Washington Post, Mar 2) has a systemic knock-on: without insurance, institutional lenders cannot finance vessel charters; cargo cannot move; the disruption propagates from oil to dry bulk and container shipping within 7–10 days if unresolved
  • Gulf state sovereign risk: Qatar's force majeure and Bahrain's proximity to Iranian missile range create sovereign credit stress — RAND flags Gulf bond spreads are beginning to price in a low-probability but non-trivial scenario of Gulf state infrastructure strikes in Phase III
  • Congressional war powers clock: Under the War Powers Resolution, the administration has 60 days from hostilities commencement before Congressional authorization is required; RAND forecasts intense Congressional pressure beginning Week 3 that will shape the negotiating timeline and may force a premature ceasefire bid that undermines the Oman framework

JINSA & Alma Center — Targeting and Nuclear Assessments

JINSA: Strike Assessment

JINSA's full strike target analysis (Feb 28) identifies confirmed hits on 14 of 19 priority targets in the initial wave. The five unconfirmed targets are all underground or hardened: Fordow, Pickaxe Mountain deep facility, IRGC Intelligence Command Bunker (Lavasan), Quds Force Operational HQ (Shiraz), and a classified ballistic missile command post. JINSA assesses that strikes succeeded at the "degradation" mission but not the "destruction" mission — Iran's nuclear program is set back 2–4 years, not terminated. JINSA recommends a Phase II targeting package focused on surviving hardened sites, employing additional B-2 sorties with GBU-57s from continental US bases if Diego Garcia access is not restored. — Source: JINSA, Feb 28, 2026

Alma Center: Nuclear & Missile Rebuild Timeline

Israel's Alma Research and Education Center (February 2026 assessment) tracks Iran's pre-strike nuclear and missile production pace. Alma's key finding: Iran had been accelerating toward 90% "weapons-grade" enrichment at Pickaxe Mountain specifically because it was below the publicly known enrichment sites and thus may have been deprioritized in US targeting. Post-strike, if the facility survives, Alma estimates Iran could produce sufficient fissile material for one device within 4–6 months using existing stocks plus ongoing production at the surviving site. This is the single most time-sensitive analytical question of the post-strike environment. — Source: Alma Center, Feb 2026
