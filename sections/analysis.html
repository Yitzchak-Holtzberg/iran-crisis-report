CSIS

Center for Strategic & International Studies

📋 Epic Fury: The Campaign Against Iran's Missile & Nuclear Infrastructure (Updated Mar 7)

CSIS' multi-track analysis of Operation Epic Fury now reflects Week 2 operations. Nuclear targets: Strikes targeted leadership, military installations, missile production sites, and the remnants of Iran's nuclear program from Day 1. Natanz enrichment hall surface structures destroyed; Fordow underground facility struck with penetration depth disputed; Isfahan research reactor damaged. The Mount Kolang Gaz La facility south of Natanz was struck for the first time on Mar 6 (ISW/ISIS, Mar 7). CSIS assesses Iran retains dispersed enriched uranium stocks and can reconstitute 90% enrichment capability within 6–18 months if political authority survives. Military objectives (per Trump's Feb 28 address): (1) prevent nuclear weapon acquisition, (2) destroy missile arsenal and production, (3) degrade proxy networks, (4) annihilate Iran's navy. CENTCOM now reports 3,000+ targets struck and 43 warships destroyed — roughly doubling the Mar 4 figures. — Source: CSIS, Mar 2026; CENTCOM, ISW/ISIS, Mar 6–7

💰 $3.7 Billion: The Cost of Epic Fury's First 100 Hours (CSIS, Mar 6)

CSIS' cost analysis reveals the campaign's sustainability challenge: the first 100 hours cost an estimated $3.7 billion — $891 million per day. Of this, $3.5 billion was unbudgeted; Congress will need new appropriations if operations continue for weeks. Air-defense interceptors alone account for ~$1.7 billion of initial costs. CSIS notes that historically, air campaigns settle into a more sustainable pace as targets require reassessment and crews need rest — but future costs depend on Iranian retaliation intensity. The IRGC's claim of 6-month readiness (Bloomberg, Mar 7) signals Tehran intends to impose sustained attrition costs. — Source: CSIS, Mar 6, 2026; Bloomberg, Mar 7

ISW

Institute for the Study of War — Morning Special Report

Iran Update Morning Special Report, March 7, 2026

ISW-CTP's March 7 morning report reveals the combined force is systematically dismantling Iran's domestic control apparatus alongside its military infrastructure. Key findings: (1) Iran continued drone and ballistic missile attacks on Gulf states despite Pezeshkian's suspension order — two drones struck UAE territory including Dubai International Airport; Saudi Arabia intercepted 8 drones and 1 ballistic missile; a missile targeting Prince Sultan Airbase fell in an uninhabited area. (2) Basij base targeting campaign: the combined force has struck 9 of 23 Basij regional bases in Tehran, including the 27th Mohammad Rasoul Ollah Provincial Unit HQ — the IRGC's main unit in the capital responsible for protest suppression. (3) LEC headquarters struck in Kurdish-populated western Iran — provincial HQs for Kermanshah, West Azerbaijan, Kurdistan, and possibly Ilam, degrading internal security in ethnically restive areas. (4) Air dominance extending into central Iran: two Artesh airbases struck in Esfahan Province. (5) Mount Kolang Gaz La nuclear facility struck for the first time — vehicle destroyed, possibly air-defense related; this facility had been hardened in recent weeks in preparation for strikes. (6) Six defense-industrial sites struck including Defense Industries Organization (nuclear/missile development), Shiraz Electronics Industries (air defense), and Jahan Electric Industrial Park (nuclear program). — Source: ISW-CTP, Mar 7, 2026

Carnegie

Carnegie Endowment for International Peace

📋 Who Will Be Iran's Next Supreme Leader? (Eric Lob, Mar 6)

Carnegie scholar Eric Lob provides the most detailed analysis of the succession contest. The Assembly of Experts is moving to elect a new supreme leader to preserve regime continuity amid war, but faces extraordinary obstacles: Israel bombed the Assembly's building in Qom during its first meeting on Tuesday and threatened to strike again if a successor is named. Trump insisted he is "going to choose" Iran's next leader (Reuters, Mar 5). Despite this, the Assembly appears determined to carry out its constitutional duty. Frontrunners: Ayatollah Alireza Arafi — a hardline cleric, Khamenei loyalist, Guardian Council member, head of Iran's seminaries, who attained ayatollah rank with strong religious credentials; and Mojtaba Khamenei — the late leader's son, with deep IRGC/Basij ties but no public office and only mid-ranking clerical status (Hojjat al-Eslam). Lob concludes: "If the succession process can be carried out to its logical conclusion, it would probably bring a hardliner into power." Khamenei stacked the Assembly with hardliners through 2024 disqualifications; moderates like Rouhani and Hassan Khomeini are unlikely to be considered. — Source: Carnegie Endowment (Lob), Mar 6, 2026

Carnegie: Succession Dynamics (Mar 7)

  • Arafi — the institutional candidate: Ayatollah rank, 20+ publications, Guardian Council and Assembly of Experts member, head of seminaries; entered Assembly via Khamenei-arranged legal loophole in 2022 — signaling he was the preferred successor
  • Mojtaba — the dynastic gamble: Deep IRGC/Basij coordination since 2009; carries the Khamenei name but hereditary succession is "anathema to the revolution"; would need constitutional amendment to elevate his clerical rank (as was done for his father in 1989)
  • Israeli disruption strategy: Bombing the Assembly building and threatening follow-up strikes is designed to prevent succession from stabilizing the regime — but may rally hardliners around urgency
  • Interim Leadership Council functioning: Pezeshkian (reformist), Mohseni-Eje'i (hardliner), and Arafi (hardliner) — a 2-to-1 hardline tilt that previews the likely direction

Carnegie: Broader Analysis — Multiple Perspectives (Mar 7)

RAND

RAND Corporation — Succession Scenarios & Strategic Risk

📈 Who — or What — Will Replace Iran's Supreme Leader? (RAND, Mar 2026)

RAND identifies four succession scenarios, noting this transition is fundamentally unlike the managed 1989 handoff — it occurs amid domestic unrest, economic crisis, and unprecedented military pressure. Scenario 1 — "Digging In": A senior cleric approved by the Assembly maintains the existing system with minimal changes — the status quo continuation. Scenario 2 — "Cut and Run": Key leaders flee, as Assad did from Syria — low probability but not zero given the intensity of strikes. Scenario 3 — "Suppression and Succession": The regime becomes more reliant on the IRGC, producing a quasi-military junta with weaker clerical legitimacy — potentially more threatening as it stifles dissent while doubling down on resistance. Scenario 4 — Popular Uprising: The population succeeds in revolution — but it remains unclear who they would support and whether they could prevail without a military institution switching sides. The Basij base targeting campaign (ISW, Mar 7) directly enables Scenario 4 by degrading the regime's protest-suppression capacity. — Source: RAND, Mar 2026

RAND: Scenario Assessment — Week 2 Update (Mar 7)

  • Scenario 3 ("Suppression + Succession") is now the active track: Mojtaba/Arafi frontrunner status + IRGC consolidation + Pezeshkian as civilian front = quasi-junta in formation
  • Scenario 4 enablers strengthening: 9/23 Basij bases in Tehran destroyed; LEC HQs hit in Kurdish areas; currency collapse continuing — the regime's capacity to suppress popular unrest is materially degrading
  • Scenario 2 risk non-zero: As strikes expand to 3,000+ targets and the IRGC's claim of 6-month readiness is tested, elite defection calculus shifts
  • Combined force appears to be shaping toward Scenario 4: Targeting Basij/LEC while leaving civilian infrastructure (relatively) intact suggests a strategy of enabling popular uprising rather than destroying the state

Economic & Hormuz Risk — Week 2 (Mar 7)

  • Hormuz functionally closed without mines: Tanker traffic down 90%+; Iran declared strait closed and attacked ships; stopped short of mining — but the effect is the same (CFR, Mar 6)
  • Oil prices elevated but not at crisis levels yet: Iran's own exports halted; 20% of global oil/LNG transits Hormuz; Trump's DFC insurance/Navy escort offer is untested
  • IRGC 6-month claim vs. $891M/day US cost: Both sides face attrition — the question is which political system absorbs the cost better
  • Congressional pressure building: House narrowly rejected war-powers resolution; rising US casualties and costs will produce another vote
  • Cyber risk remains elevated: Iranian threat actors retain capability for critical-infrastructure targeting as kinetic pressure mounts

Critical Fault Lines — Analyst Disagreement (Mar 7)

  • Deal vs. regime change? Brookings (Maloney) assesses a deal is still more likely than regime change; CFR (Froman) notes it depends entirely on long-term outcomes; Trump's "unconditional surrender" demand makes diplomacy structurally impossible in the near term
  • Is the IRGC fracturing or consolidating? ISW notes Pezeshkian's halt order was contradicted by IRGC action within hours — suggesting either autonomous IRGC operations or deliberate deception. RAND's Scenario 3 (quasi-junta) assumes consolidation; Scenario 4 (uprising) assumes fracture. The ground truth remains unclear.
  • Can Basij degradation enable revolution? The combined force is clearly targeting protest-suppression infrastructure (ISW, Mar 7). RAND argues this enables popular uprising — but Brookings warns that without an embedded transitional authority, collapse risks Libya-style chaos, not democratic transition
  • Hormuz endgame: Tanker traffic has collapsed 90%+ without mines being laid. CFR notes Trump is offering insurance and escorts, but the question is whether Iran will cross the mining threshold — which would escalate from a de-facto to a permanent closure with global recession implications
