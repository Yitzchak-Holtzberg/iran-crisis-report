Brookings: "After the Strike" — Key Arguments (Maloney et al., Mar 2–3, 2026)

Brookings' Suzanne Maloney, Michael O'Hanlon, Mara Karlin, Vanda Felbab-Brown and colleagues published "After the strike: The danger of war in Iran" (Brookings, Mar 2–3, 2026). The central Maloney argument: this is Iran's "Khamenei raft" moment — as momentous as the Shah's departure in 1979 — but hope may be short-lived. "Deeply embedded networks and institutions that have underpinned the Islamic Republic for nearly half a century ensure that, at least in the near term, the vestiges of the power structure continue to hold an overwhelming advantage over any challengers." Maloney notes Iran's survival strategy: escalating on neighbors' energy and economic infrastructure to create diplomatic pressure — the same playbook Tehran has used in every existential crisis. O'Hanlon flags the sustainability question: the US force posture is more suited for a multiday bombing campaign than a prolonged conflict — questions remain about munition supply, regional allied support, and readiness cost for other global priorities. Karlin notes Iranian missiles and drones have attacked at least nine countries as of Mar 3, indicating devolved command and control under the bombardment. Felbab-Brown and colleagues flag the protracted instability risk: even with strikes degrading both military and internal security apparatus, the core regime retains significant control; the international community is watching for a diplomatic opening, but for now the emphasis remains on crisis management, not resolution.

Next Steps (Brookings, Mar 3): Maloney warns the "hope may be short-lived" framing means the US must use the diplomatic window (including Vienna talks, Al Jazeera / IAEA, Mar 4) while the regime is under maximum pressure — before the "deeply embedded networks" stabilize around a new authority. O'Hanlon flags the War Powers Resolution clock and the sustainability constraint as the primary US domestic pressure points: Congressional intervention could foreclose diplomatic options before Vienna talks can produce a framework. The 6 US deaths are the trigger variable that accelerates Congressional scrutiny. — Source: Brookings, Maloney et al., Mar 2–3, 2026; OCHA, Red Crescent, Al Jazeera, IAEA, Mar 3–4, 2026

CFR

Council on Foreign Relations — Global Consequences Assessment

🌎 After Khamenei: Leadership Transition Scenarios (CFR Report, 2026)

CFR's "After Khamenei: Planning for Iran's Leadership Transition" report lays out three plausible succession pathways directly relevant to the current situation. Scenario 1 — Hard Right Shift (most likely, per CFR): Explicit assumption of formal leadership by Iran's military-security apparatus — the IRGC formalizing the shift in power balance underway for decades. CFR's report notes: "A regime dominated by the IRGC would formalize a shift in the balance of power that has been underway since the end of the Iran-Iraq War." Mojtaba Khamenei — who studied under extremist hard-liner Ayatollah Taqi Mesbah Yazdi and wields influence through IRGC ties — "could emerge as a central figure in a militarized Islamic Republic." Other key figures: Mohammad Baqr Qalibaf (speaker of parliament), Ali Shamkhani (head of Defense Council), and Ali Larijani (chair of Supreme National Security Commission). CFR models the outcome as a Pakistan/Egypt-style highly repressive state relying on force rather than ideological narratives — with some reform flexibility on cultural issues (e.g., hijab enforcement) but no meaningful democratic or economic reform. Scenario 2 — Regime Collapse: Progressive devolution of state authority "gradually and then all at once" — not inconceivable given Syria's trajectory; if it unfolds, any interregnum could be "disruptive and destabilizing well beyond Iran's borders" with a likely military takeover resolving the vacuum. CFR's critical next-step finding: whichever scenario unfolds, the US must have a plan for both — the Hard Right Shift (IRGC-dominated Iran that may still accept a nuclear verification deal) and Regime Collapse (requiring international coordination to prevent humanitarian catastrophe and nuclear material insecurity). — Source: CFR, "After Khamenei: Planning for Iran's Leadership Transition," 2026; Al Jazeera, IAEA, Mar 3–4, 2026

CFR: Hard Right Shift Scenario — Implications (Mar 4)

  • IRGC formalization is now CFR's most likely path: The Assembly of Experts naming Mojtaba (Reuters, AP, Al Jazeera, Mar 3) is consistent with CFR's "Hard Right Shift" scenario; Mojtaba's IRGC ties and background under Ayatollah Mesbah Yazdi make him a credible figurehead for a militarized Islamic Republic; CFR identifies Qalibaf, Shamkhani, and Larijani as the key power-behind-the-throne figures whose behavior will determine whether the junta stabilizes or fractures
  • Pakistan/Egypt model — what it means for diplomacy: CFR's Hard Right Shift model predicts a state that "could prove more pragmatic around some divisive cultural policies" but "unlikely to embrace the West or implement any meaningful democratic reforms" — this means nuclear verification deals are possible in this scenario, but sanctions removal as an incentive for structural change is not; Vienna talks should be designed for this model, not for a liberalizing transition
  • Mojtaba's legitimacy deficit — CFR flagged this scenario: CFR's report pre-identified the constitutional problem: an IRGC-backed succession would rely on "IRGC coercion rather than clerical consensus" — Ayatollah Arafi's public posture in the next 24–48 hours is the specific indicator CFR's model identifies for whether the Hard Right Shift stabilizes or triggers clerical pushback
  • Alliance fracture — European public opinion hardening: Germany, France, and Turkey have refused to endorse the operation; with 1,400+ civilian dead (OCHA/Red Crescent, Mar 4) and 6 US deaths, European public opinion is hardening; CFR's succession analysis notes any IRGC-dominated Iran will face renewed international isolation, reinforcing the case for Vienna talks as the only available multilateral engagement mechanism

CFR: Regime Collapse Scenario & Policy Next Steps (Mar 4)

  • Regime Collapse — CFR's second scenario (lower probability, higher risk): "Progressive devolution of state authority amid persistent social instability" — CFR explicitly states this "cannot be considered inconceivable" given Syria's trajectory; for US planners, regime collapse requires pre-positioned coordination with Gulf states, IAEA, and regional partners to prevent a humanitarian catastrophe and nuclear material security gap that would dwarf the Vienna talks agenda
  • Vienna talks must plan for both scenarios simultaneously: CFR's framework means the US must arrive at Vienna with two parallel tracks: (a) a nuclear verification framework viable with an IRGC-dominated Iran (Hard Right Shift); (b) a nuclear material security arrangement activated in the event of Regime Collapse; the IAEA must be prepared for both; failure to plan for Regime Collapse creates catastrophic exposure even if Vienna talks succeed in the near term
  • Sanctions architecture as Vienna leverage (CFR): CFR notes the IRGC's Hard Right Shift model explicitly includes a pathway where it "seeks to quickly gain the support of the Iranian people by showing greater flexibility for a deal with the United States in exchange for an economic boost in the form of sanctions relief" — this is the Vienna talks incentive structure; the US must offer credible sanctions relief commitment to activate that IRGC pathway
  • Ethnic/minority instability risk in Regime Collapse (CFR): CFR warns regime collapse "could activate aspirations among some of the country's minority groups — Iranian Kurdish or Baloch citizens" with cross-border instability implications; this is the Kurdish spoiler risk Carnegie also flags; the Vienna talks must have Gulf state backing (Saudi $500M+ pledge) to make the stability argument credible to Iranian interlocutors who fear collapse more than they desire change
