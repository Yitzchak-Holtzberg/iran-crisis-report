CSIS

Center for Strategic & International Studies

📋 Operation Epic Fury and the Remnants of Iran's Nuclear Program (Feb 28)

CSIS concludes that strikes have degraded but not eliminated Iran's nuclear capability. Key findings: Natanz enrichment hall surface structures destroyed; Fordow underground facility struck, penetration depth disputed; Isfahan research reactor damaged; Pickaxe Mountain facility south of Natanz — BDA inconclusive. CSIS assesses Iran retains 600–900 kg of 20–60% enriched uranium at dispersed sites, and can reconstitute 90% enrichment capability within 6–18 months if political authority survives. CSIS recommends a verified "zero enrichment" deal within the next 30-day window as the only path that forecloses a Phase III campaign. — Source: CSIS, Feb 28, 2026

⚓ US Military in the Middle East: The Numbers Behind Trump's Threats (Feb 24)

CSIS pre-strike force assessment: three carrier strike groups, 150+ aircraft surged, 35,000+ troops positioned — CSIS described this as "the largest naval force in the region since Operation Iraqi Freedom (2003)." CSIS analysts noted the force is sized for punitive strikes, not sustained occupation, and lacks the ground component for extended campaign logistics. Post-strike, this assessment underpins Phase II questions: Can the US sustain a second strike wave? CSIS says yes — for 7–10 days of high-intensity strikes before carrier strike groups require replenishment cycles. — Source: CSIS, Feb 24, 2026

CSIS Phase II Outlook — Key Questions

  • Nuclear mission completion: BDA on Pickaxe Mountain and Fordow underground centrifuge halls within 72 hours will determine whether Phase III strikes are politically necessary
  • Succession vacuum: CSIS identifies the 48–72 hour window after Khamenei's death as a decisive diplomatic opening — the only period before IRGC factions consolidate a new authority structure
  • Hormuz closure risk: CSIS models a 14-day closure driving WTI above $120/barrel; 30-day closure triggering global recession conditions. US strategic petroleum reserves provide 30–45 days of buffer
  • Phase III trigger: If enrichment capability survives at Pickaxe Mountain, CSIS assesses Trump administration faces binary choice — accept a nuclear-capable Iran or launch a third strike wave within 30 days

CSIS Policy Recommendations (Post-Strike)

  • Open a ceasefire channel immediately — the 48-hour succession window is the only period where a political deal can be struck without a permanent counterpart; Oman is the only credible intermediary
  • Secure enriched uranium stockpiles — CSIS calls for pre-positioning IAEA rapid-response teams within 24 hours of regime fracture to prevent nuclear material from becoming a bargaining chip or proliferation vector
  • Do not publicly commit to regime change — CSIS analysts warn that explicit US embrace of Pahlavi as transitional authority may consolidate IRGC resistance rather than fracture it
  • Gulf state economic guarantees — Saudi and UAE reconstruction pledges are the financial foundation of any managed transition; CSIS recommends US facilitate Gulf financial commitment before any recognition of transitional authority
ISW

Institute for the Study of War — Special Report

Iran Update: US and Israeli Strikes & Axis of Resistance Responses (Feb 28)

ISW's special report concludes that Operation Epic Fury represents the structural end of the "Axis of Resistance" as a coherent strategic concept. With Hezbollah's command structure destroyed in 2025, Syria lost, the Iraq–Lebanon land bridge severed, and now Iran's homeland under sustained bombardment, the proxy network has lost its central node. ISW finds that Iran's ~170 ballistic missile salvo on Feb 28 exhausted Iran's primary retaliatory tool without achieving strategic effect — interception rates above 90% across Arrow 3, Iron Dome, Patriot, and THAAD systems meant the barrage was costly and militarily inconclusive. — Source: ISW, Feb 28, 2026

ISW: Post-Strike Military Assessment

  • IRGC command decapitation: ISW maps the command vacuum — six IRGC corps (Aerospace, Ground, Navy, Quds, Intelligence, Basij) now lack unified strategic direction; cross-corps coordination degraded for an estimated 72–96 hours
  • Remaining strike capacity: Iran retains an estimated 1,300–1,400 ballistic missiles after the Feb 28 salvo; mobile TEL launchers dispersed in mountain tunnels remain largely intact and operational
  • Hormuz mining threat: ISW assesses IRGC Navy fast-attack vessels and mine-laying capability is fully intact — was not a primary Phase I target. Hormuz closure via mining is the most significant remaining Iranian escalation option
  • Drone stockpile unknown: Shahed-136/238 production facilities not confirmed destroyed; ISW assesses Iran retains significant drone capacity for asymmetric follow-on attacks against Gulf infrastructure

ISW: Political-Military Outlook

  • Succession contest is the critical variable: ISW identifies the race between Mojtaba Khamenei (junta path) and the protest movement (collapse path) as the decisive dynamic of the next 72 hours; no external actor controls this outcome
  • Proxy network disintegration: ISW expects Houthi, Iraqi PMF, and Lebanese Hezbollah remnants to reduce operations significantly within 7–14 days as Iranian funding chains and command channels collapse
  • Russian and Chinese response: ISW predicts Moscow and Beijing will signal quiet support for IRGC junta continuity within 24–48 hours; neither wants Iranian chaos near their energy and trade infrastructure
Carnegie

Carnegie Endowment for International Peace

Carnegie's Karim Sadjadpour, the leading US analyst of Iranian domestic politics, frames the post-Khamenei succession as "the most dangerous transition in Middle Eastern politics since 1979." Carnegie's analysis prioritizes the Mojtaba Khamenei variable — describing him as a figure who was "systematically positioned to inherit his father's mantle" but who faces a legitimacy deficit that may prove fatal to the junta scenario.

The Succession Analysis (Sadjadpour)

  • Mojtaba's legitimacy problem: He is not a senior cleric and cannot claim the velayat-e faqih (guardianship of the jurist) that underpins the Islamic Republic's constitutional legitimacy; any attempt to rule would be transparently dynastic — the opposite of the 1979 revolution's founding ideology
  • Assembly of Experts: The constitutional body responsible for selecting a new Supreme Leader is 88 members, median age 72, and split between hardliners and pragmatists. Carnegie assesses Mojtaba can secure a slim majority but not the two-thirds needed for a politically legitimate selection
  • The reformist wildcard: Carnegie identifies a small bloc of reformist clerics inside the Assembly who may see the post-strike chaos as their last opportunity to assert influence; their alignment could determine whether the succession produces a junta or a compromise figure

Carnegie: Democratic Transition Conditions

  • Five simultaneous conditions now exist that have never previously been true: (1) IRGC economic base under destruction; (2) protest movement has a named leader for the first time in 46 years; (3) CIA operational defection channel is live; (4) Trump administration back-channel to Pahlavi confirmed; (5) Khamenei dead, removing the authority figure who could order unified resistance
  • The conversion problem: Carnegie warns that conditions ≠ outcome — Iran's opposition has had favorable conditions before (2009, 2019) and failed to convert them; the difference this time is the external military dimension, which changes the IRGC's internal calculation
  • Kurdish fracture as spoiler: Carnegie assesses the Pahlavi–Kurdish dissident split as the single most likely obstacle to a coordinated transition; PJAK in the northwest could open a low-grade insurgency within days of any transitional authority announcement
Brookings

Brookings Institution

The Day-After Problem

Brookings' Iran analysts draw the sharpest historical parallel: Iraq 2003. The US dismantled a state apparatus in 21 days and spent 20 years managing the consequences. Iran has four times Iraq's population, an intact nuclear program, and a protest movement that has named an external leader rather than emerged organically from within. Brookings flags three critical planning failures it assesses the US has not addressed: (1) no post-strike civil administration plan for 31 provinces with IRGC-dominated economies; (2) nuclear material chain-of-custody gap — 1,000+ kg of enriched uranium at dispersed sites with no US/IAEA pre-positioned rapid-response teams; (3) economic reconstitution financing — $100B+ in frozen assets cannot be unlocked without Congressional action and EU agreement that have not yet been initiated. Brookings does not assess that military strikes alone produce a stable outcome without these three elements.

Atl. Council

Atlantic Council

The Regional Domino Question

Atlantic Council's Middle East program focuses on second-order regional effects. Key assessments: Gulf states (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Bahrain) are simultaneously victims of Iranian missile strikes and potential beneficiaries of regime change — their posture is to absorb Iranian retaliation while privately hoping for Iranian regime collapse. AC identifies Jordan as the decisive swing state: Jordan denied US airspace for strike operations (limiting the Fordow BDA) but intercepted Iranian missiles over its territory — it is neither fully aligned with the US nor with Iran, and its economic dependence on Gulf state financing makes it ultimately malleable. AC warns that a prolonged conflict without a defined political endgame risks Palestinian political destabilization — the Gaza cease-fire is now under extreme pressure as the Iran crisis consumes US diplomatic bandwidth.

JINSA / Alma Center

JINSA & Alma Center — Targeting and Nuclear Assessments

JINSA: Strike Assessment

JINSA's full strike target analysis (Feb 28) identifies confirmed hits on 14 of 19 priority targets in the initial wave. The five unconfirmed targets are all underground or hardened: Fordow, Pickaxe Mountain deep facility, IRGC Intelligence Command Bunker (Lavasan), Quds Force Operational HQ (Shiraz), and a classified ballistic missile command post. JINSA assesses that strikes succeeded at the "degradation" mission but not the "destruction" mission — Iran's nuclear program is set back 2–4 years, not terminated. JINSA recommends a Phase II targeting package focused on surviving hardened sites, employing additional B-2 sorties with GBU-57s from continental US bases if Diego Garcia access is not restored. — Source: JINSA, Feb 28, 2026

Alma Center: Nuclear & Missile Rebuild Timeline

Israel's Alma Research and Education Center (February 2026 assessment) tracks Iran's pre-strike nuclear and missile production pace. Alma's key finding: Iran had been accelerating toward 90% "weapons-grade" enrichment at Pickaxe Mountain specifically because it was below the publicly known enrichment sites and thus may have been deprioritized in US targeting. Post-strike, if the facility survives, Alma estimates Iran could produce sufficient fissile material for one device within 4–6 months using existing stocks plus ongoing production at the surviving site. This is the single most time-sensitive analytical question of the post-strike environment. — Source: Alma Center, Feb 2026

📋 Analyst Consensus: The Three Open Questions (Mar 1–3)

Across CSIS, ISW, Carnegie, Brookings, Atlantic Council, JINSA, and Alma — four analytical conclusions are broadly shared, and three questions remain genuinely open:

Shared Conclusions (Analyst Consensus)

  • Strikes degraded but did not destroy Iran's nuclear capability — reconstitution is possible within 6–18 months
  • Iran's missile salvo (170 missiles) was absorbed without strategic effect; Iran's primary retaliatory tool is effectively spent
  • The Axis of Resistance has lost its strategic coherence — proxy networks will continue operating at reduced capacity without central Iranian direction
  • The 48–72 hour window after Khamenei's death is the most important political moment in Iranian history since 1979

Open Questions (Genuine Uncertainty)

  • Pickaxe Mountain BDA: Did the bunker-busters penetrate the deep facility? The answer determines whether Phase III is politically necessary (CSIS, JINSA, Alma)
  • Succession outcome: Junta (Mojtaba / Salami) or collapse (protest movement + Pahlavi transition)? The race between these two is unresolved and uncontrolled by any external actor (Carnegie, ISW)
  • Hormuz escalation: Will the IRGC Navy mine the strait? This is the single most consequential economic decision remaining in Iranian hands — and IRGC Navy capability is fully intact (ISW, Brookings, CSIS)
