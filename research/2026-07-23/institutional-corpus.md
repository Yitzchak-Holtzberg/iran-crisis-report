## Cross-institution extraction

### What is established across the corpus

1. **Operational damage and strategic success are different questions.** CSIS, RAND, IISS, CTP–ISW, RUSI, Chatham House, and Atlantic Council all accept that Iran's conventional military infrastructure suffered major damage. None can establish from public evidence that the damage removed Iran's ability to impose regional costs or produced the political outcome sought by Washington and Israel.
2. **Iran adapted faster than the original strategy anticipated.** Carnegie, CSIS, IISS, CTP–ISW, RUSI, and Chatham House describe a shift from a prewar deterrence system centered on missiles and partner forces toward a wartime system that combines dispersed missiles and drones, pressure on Gulf infrastructure, maritime coercion, economic leverage, and linkage between theaters.
3. **Hormuz became the conflict's central bargaining mechanism.** Institutions differ over whether Iran has "won" control, but there is broad agreement that the military condition of the channel is only one part of the problem. Mines, coastal launchers, drones, small craft, insurance, crew willingness, vessel routing, legal exposure, and political agreements all affect whether passage is usable.
4. **The June memorandum was a pause, not a settlement.** CSIS, CFR, Brookings, Atlantic Council, IISS, Chatham House, RUSI, and CTP–ISW identify unresolved or ambiguously sequenced questions involving navigation, sanctions relief, the U.S. blockade, Iran's nuclear material, enrichment, inspections, Hezbollah, and wider regional security. The July return to sustained strikes is later evidence against optimistic readings of the memorandum.
5. **The nuclear result is not publicly knowable with confidence.** Physical damage is visible at several declared facilities, but IAEA access is insufficient to verify the size, composition, or location of the enriched-uranium stockpile or whether enrichment-related activity has stopped. Analysts who infer denuclearization from strike damage exceed the public evidence.
6. **The regime did not collapse under military pressure.** RAND, IISS, CTP–ISW, Carnegie, and Atlantic Council disagree about long-term resilience but broadly reject the assumption that decapitation would automatically generate a democratic transition. Wartime repression, communications restrictions, nationalist reaction, IRGC influence, and opposition fragmentation all complicate the picture.
7. **Gulf policy is neither simple alignment nor neutrality.** The attacks created stronger incentives for shared defense and opposition to Iranian coercion, while the decision to begin the war without meaningful Gulf consultation and the exposure of host states created deeper doubts about dependence on Washington. Both movements are occurring simultaneously.
8. **The cost extends well beyond oil.** The corpus identifies LNG, refining, fertilizer, food affordability, shipping, insurance, aviation, tourism, migration, health systems, water infrastructure, military stockpiles, and fiscal and legal constraints as part of the crisis picture.

### Important disagreements to preserve

- **Can sustained military pressure break Iran's Hormuz leverage?** CTP–ISW and some Washington Institute work argue that stronger pressure is required to prevent a new Iranian navigational order. IISS, RUSI, Brookings, and much of the CSIS/CFR corpus emphasize the impracticality of eliminating dispersed threats without a political agreement.
- **Did Iran emerge strategically stronger?** CFR, some CSIS authors, CTP–ISW, and Atlantic Council contributors argue that the June terms validated Iranian leverage. RAND and other Atlantic Council authors give more weight to durable Iranian losses and long-run vulnerability. The July relapse shows that neither side converted its leverage into a stable settlement.
- **Is regime consolidation durable?** IISS and CTP–ISW see greater IRGC weight in decision-making. RUSI and Carnegie stress that wartime cohesion does not solve Iran's damaged infrastructure, water, health, inflation, reconstruction, legitimacy, or succession problems.
- **Would additional strikes improve the nuclear outcome?** Some security-policy authors support continuing kinetic denial. Chatham House, IISS, and arms-control specialists emphasize that attacks can destroy equipment while degrading verification, increasing incentives to disperse activity, and leaving material and knowledge unresolved.
- **How should the opposition be understood?** Advocacy sources often present a ready alternative government. RAND, AP reporting, Iran specialists, and the opposition's own disputes support a narrower conclusion: opposition to the Islamic Republic is broad, but organization, internal support, territorial reach, and agreement on a transition mechanism are not publicly established.

## Initial proposition map

These are analytical propositions to test, not conclusions.

### 1. Operational degradation was substantial, but durability is uncertain

Support:

- RAND
- CSIS reconstitution study
- early IISS operational analysis
- CTP–ISW operational updates

Questions:

- How much relies on belligerent claims?
- Which production, command, launcher, naval, and nuclear effects are independently observable?

### 2. War termination, not target destruction, became the central strategic failure

Support:

- RAND's “dilemma” framing
- CSIS on political endurance
- IISS on mutually worse strategic outcomes
- Atlantic Council's “misguided war, flawed deal” argument

### 3. Hormuz became Iran's strongest surviving coercive instrument

Broad agreement:

- CSIS
- IISS
- Brookings
- Carnegie
- CFR
- CTP–ISW
- Atlantic Council

Disagreement:

- whether military pressure can break the leverage;
- whether Iran achieved de facto control;
- whether the arrangement is temporary bargaining leverage or a durable strategic precedent;
- and whether continued U.S. blockade pressure improves or worsens the end state.

### 4. Conventional naval destruction did not eliminate maritime coercion

Broad agreement:

- IISS
- Brookings
- CSIS
- Atlantic Council

Key distinction:

- fleet destruction;
- coastal missiles and drones;
- mines and small craft;
- commercial risk tolerance;
- insurance availability;
- crew willingness;
- legal authority;
- and political coalition willingness.

### 5. The war may have consolidated rather than destabilized the regime

Support:

- IISS “Iran’s Militarised Islamic Republic”

Counterquestions:

- What is known about elite cohesion?
- What evidence exists outside regime-controlled visibility?
