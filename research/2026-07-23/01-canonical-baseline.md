# Canonical Baseline

As of: 2026-07-23 ET  
Status: page-rewrite gate  
Use: common factual and chronological spine for every page

## Conflict phases

### Phase 0 — Conditions before the 2026 war

- The 2015 nuclear agreement had already collapsed as an effective constraint.
- Iran's declared enrichment program, highly enriched uranium, unresolved safeguards questions, and reduced IAEA access created a serious verification crisis.
- The 2025 U.S.–Israel strikes damaged declared nuclear infrastructure but did not produce verified accounting for Iran's enriched material or end the nuclear dispute.
- Iran's regional deterrence system had already been weakened by the post-October 2023 wars, but Hezbollah, Iraqi groups, the Houthis, missiles, drones, and maritime tools remained relevant.
- Nationwide protests beginning in late 2025 and January 2026 were met with lethal repression and a sweeping communications shutdown. Their true scale and casualty toll remain difficult to verify.

### Phase 1 — Opening campaign, 28 February to early April

- U.S. Central Command says Operation Epic Fury began at 01:15 on 28 February 2026. The opening campaign targeted leadership, air defense, missile, drone, naval, security, and supporting infrastructure.
- Ayatollah Ali Khamenei was killed in the opening strikes. An interim leadership mechanism operated before the Assembly of Experts selected Mojtaba Khamenei on 8 March.
- Iran responded across several lines at once: missiles and drones against U.S., Israeli, and Gulf targets; attacks on economic and civilian infrastructure; partner-force activity; and maritime coercion in and around Hormuz.
- The U.S.–Israeli campaign established a major operational advantage and destroyed or damaged important Iranian assets. Public totals remain dominated by belligerent claims and cannot be treated as a complete loss ledger.
- The humanitarian cost inside Iran became severe. OCHA's 1 May update, using Iranian and UN-system reporting with stated methodological limits, recorded at least 3,375 civilian deaths, 32,314 injuries, large-scale temporary relocation, and widespread damage to housing, schools, health, electricity, and water infrastructure.
- A temporary ceasefire began on 7 April. It stopped the initial continuous campaign but did not settle the nuclear, maritime, sanctions, regional, or security questions.

### Phase 2 — Coercive pause and blockade, April to mid-June

- The conflict shifted from continuous large-scale air operations toward blockade enforcement, episodic strikes, interception, negotiation, and repeated maritime incidents.
- The U.S. imposed a blockade on shipping to and from Iranian ports. Iran sought to shape or control traffic through Hormuz using threats, route demands, attacks, and claims of administrative authority.
- Shipping and energy disruption became the primary strategic pressure on all sides. IEA data show that Gulf oil exports and production remained far below prewar levels even when traffic improved.
- Iran used the pause to preserve and begin reconstituting dispersed capabilities. Public evidence does not support a clean inventory of what remained or was rebuilt.

### Phase 3 — June memorandum and partial reopening

- The United States and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding on 17 June after mediation involving regional states.
- The memorandum temporarily reduced hostilities, lifted or relaxed some U.S. restrictions, and enabled a partial recovery in traffic. It deferred, ambiguously sequenced, or left unresolved the future status of Hormuz, sanctions relief, the U.S. blockade, Iran's nuclear material and enrichment, IAEA access, Hezbollah, and wider regional arrangements.
- IEA reported that total Gulf oil exports, including bypass routes, rose to 16.1 million barrels per day in June, still below the prewar average of 24 million barrels per day.
- Commercial recovery remained fragile because physical passage, attack risk, insurance, crew willingness, routing, mines, port and refinery damage, legal exposure, and political confidence did not recover at the same rate.

### Phase 4 — July breakdown and renewed sustained strikes

- Iranian attacks on commercial vessels and U.S. responses escalated from 7 July.
- CENTCOM resumed repeated strikes on air-defense systems, coastal radar, missile and drone capabilities, command infrastructure, logistics, and small craft. It also reimposed blockade measures.
- Iran retaliated against U.S. and regional targets and continued attacks or threats against shipping. The target set expanded to civilian and critical infrastructure on both sides.
- By 20–22 July, U.S. and Iranian attacks had become a sustained cycle. CSIS described the pattern as a self-perpetuating escalation dynamic. AP reported an eleventh consecutive U.S. strike night and continuing Iranian attacks on regional targets and shipping.
- Mediation continued, but no publicly established arrangement had resolved the immediate escalation, the maritime order, or the underlying nuclear and regional disputes as of this baseline.

## Present situation in one page

1. **Military:** The United States retains overwhelming conventional air and naval superiority. Iran's conventional military has been heavily damaged, but dispersed missiles, drones, coastal systems, mines, small craft, cyber tools, partner forces, and the ability to target exposed infrastructure remain strategically relevant.
2. **Maritime:** Neither "open" nor "closed" is adequate. Passage occurs, but volumes, routes, escort conditions, attack risk, insurance, and political authority are contested. Repeated attacks can suppress commerce without physically sealing the channel.
3. **Nuclear:** Several declared facilities are damaged, but the IAEA cannot verify the current size, composition, or location of Iran's enriched-uranium stockpile or whether enrichment-related activity has stopped. Pickaxe Mountain has not been inspected. No public evidence establishes an Iranian nuclear weapon or a current decision to build one.
4. **Iranian state:** Mojtaba Khamenei is supreme leader; President Masoud Pezeshkian, Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, senior clerics, and IRGC commander Ahmad Vahidi remain important. The exact internal balance is opaque. CTP–ISW assesses increased IRGC influence; that is an assessment, not a directly observable constitutional change.
5. **Iranian society:** War damage, repression, inflation, communications controls, water and health-system strain, displacement, and interrupted livelihoods are simultaneous pressures. These conditions can weaken legitimacy while also making collective action harder.
6. **Opposition:** Opposition to the Islamic Republic is broad and politically diverse. Reza Pahlavi has visibility and a significant diaspora base, but internal support cannot be measured reliably. Monarchists, republicans, Kurdish and other ethnic movements, labor and civil-society networks, the Woman, Life, Freedom movement, and the MEK do not share a single transition plan.
7. **Region:** Gulf states reject Iranian attacks and coercive control of Hormuz, depend on U.S. security support, and also resent their exposure to a war they did not initiate. Israel's goals overlap with Washington's on Iranian capability but diverge on war termination and regional sequencing.
8. **Diplomacy:** Negotiations are not a single track. Navigation, blockade relief, sanctions, enriched material, enrichment, inspections, Hezbollah/Lebanon, regional bases, and security guarantees are linked but not aligned.

|---|---|---|---|
| Operation Epic Fury began 28 February 2026 | Confirmed as a U.S. government statement | CENTCOM fact sheet | Attribute purpose and target claims to CENTCOM |
| Mojtaba Khamenei selected 8 March | Confirmed by Iranian announcement and AP | AP; Iranian state announcement | Do not infer how much personal control he exercises |
| U.S.–Iran memorandum signed 17 June | Confirmed | AP; official and institutional records | Describe as a memorandum and temporary framework, not peace |
| Sustained U.S. strikes resumed in July | Confirmed | CENTCOM; AP | Specify date and target categories; do not claim effectiveness without evidence |
| Iran retains ability to disrupt shipping | Confirmed by repeated incidents; degree disputed | UKMTO/JMIC, AP, CENTCOM, commercial data | Avoid "controls" unless clearly attributed |

## Claims not safe to publish as settled fact

- Exact total Iranian missiles, drones, launchers, aircraft, vessels, air-defense systems, or production sites destroyed.
- Exact remaining Iranian missile or drone inventory.
- A total count of all projectiles fired since 28 February unless categories and cutoff are stated.
- A single percentage for Strait of Hormuz traffic without source, date, direction, vessel class, and treatment of AIS-dark traffic.
- The claim that Iran is denuclearized, has resumed enrichment, has moved material to Pickaxe Mountain, or has decided to build a weapon.
- The claim that the regime is about to collapse or that wartime cohesion proves long-term stability.
- A representative measure of support inside Iran for Pahlavi or any opposition organization.
- Precise scenario probabilities.

## Review triggers

Reopen this baseline when any of the following occurs:

- a signed ceasefire or published final agreement;
- verified IAEA access to affected sites or material;
- a confirmed leadership death or constitutional transfer of authority;
- a sustained, independently measured change in Hormuz commercial traffic;
- a major ground operation or seizure of Iranian territory;
- a confirmed nuclear test or public evidence of a weaponization decision;
- verified large-scale mutiny, elite split, or nationwide protest wave;
- or a material change in U.S., Israeli, Gulf, Russian, Chinese, or partner-force participation.
