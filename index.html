Iran's Remaining Military Capability

After the June 2025 strikes: degraded but not destroyed
Operation "Epic Fury" Launched — Feb 28, 2026

The United States and Israel launched coordinated strikes against targets inside Iran as President Trump announced "major combat operations". Iran retaliated with ballistic missile barrages toward Israel and US bases in Qatar, Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE, triggering regional air defenses. Multiple oil traders temporarily halted shipments through the Strait of Hormuz amid insurance and safety concerns. — Sources: Reuters, BBC, New York Times, Feb 28

The June 2025 Israeli-US strikes dealt devastating blows to Iran's nuclear infrastructure and air defenses. But Iran retains significant conventional capabilities — and is rebuilding faster than many analysts expected, largely with Chinese technical assistance.

Degraded
  • Air defenses: Russian S-300 systems repositioned around Tehran, but radar components possibly damaged — effectiveness uncertain
  • Nuclear facilities: Natanz, Isfahan heavily damaged; centrifuge cascades reduced by ~60%
  • Air force: Aging fleet of F-4s, F-14s, MiG-29s — no match for US 5th-gen fighters
  • Command & control: Multiple IRGC senior commanders killed; communications compromised
Still Operational
  • Missiles: ~1,500 ballistic/cruise missiles, ~200 mobile launchers — hardened in dispersed mountain tunnels
  • Bavar-373: Indigenous long-range SAM system (Iran's S-300 equivalent) — unknown quantity deployed
  • Drones: Shahed-136/238 production accelerated — proven in Ukraine via Russia
  • IRGC Navy: Intact — the Strait of Hormuz force was not targeted in June strikes
  • Cyber: Offensive cyber capabilities remain fully operational

Iran's Nuclear Hardening — Feb 2026

NEW (Feb 20): Satellite imagery shows a concrete structure covered by soil was placed over the Parchin nuclear site, specifically to protect against potential US/Israeli airstrikes. — Source: The War Zone, Feb 20, 2026
The China Factor

China has emerged as Iran's primary military supply chain. Despite sanctions, Chinese companies are providing dual-use components for missile guidance, drone manufacturing, and radar systems. The "Pickaxe Mountain" deep centrifuge facility near Isfahan was reportedly built with Chinese tunneling expertise. Beijing's calculus: a weakened-but-surviving Iran keeps US military resources tied down in the Middle East — away from the Taiwan Strait.

⚡ Iran's Asymmetric Response Playbook

Iran has spent 40 years developing an asymmetric deterrence doctrine specifically designed to impose devastating costs on a superior conventional force. If attacked, Iran would not fight the US military head-to-head — it would trigger a cascading, multi-front response designed to make the cost of war intolerable.

The Missile Threat

  • Shahab-3: Range 2,000km — reaches Israel, Saudi Arabia, US Gulf bases
  • Emad/Ghadr: Precision-guided variants; accuracy improved post-2020
  • Kheibar Shekan: Hypersonic-maneuvering variant; can potentially evade Patriot/THAAD
  • Fattah-1: Claimed hypersonic glide vehicle — range 1,400km; limited inventory
  • ~200 mobile TEL launchers dispersed in mountain tunnels — pre-positioned before any strike

The Cyber Threat

  • APT33 / Elfin: IRGC-linked group with proven capability against Saudi Aramco (Shamoon 2012), US banks, Israeli infrastructure
  • APT34 / OilRig: Specializes in Gulf state energy sector penetration — has pre-positioned access
  • Iranian hackers have demonstrated SCADA/ICS attacks against water treatment and power facilities
  • Destructive wiper malware pre-positioned in adversary networks — similar to Russia's NotPetya playbook
  • An attack on Gulf financial clearing systems (SWIFT equivalents) would cause immediate global economic disruption
The Carrier Threat — Iran's Anti-Access/Area Denial

Iran has specifically invested in anti-ship capabilities designed to threaten US carriers — the platforms that would be central to any US strike. The Noor anti-ship missile (Chinese C-802 derivative, range 170km), the Qader (range 200km), and the Khalij Fars (anti-ship ballistic missile, range 300km) have all been demonstrated in live-fire drills. The IRGC Navy's swarm tactics — hundreds of fast-attack boats simultaneously targeting a carrier — are specifically designed to overwhelm close-in weapons systems. China's Feb 24 agreement to supply additional anti-ship missiles directly increases this threat. No US carrier has been tested in combat against this threat profile since the Cold War. The Pentagon acknowledges carriers may need to operate at greater stand-off distances — reducing their strike effectiveness.

The Broader Opposition Landscape

Pahlavi is the most visible face of the Iranian opposition, but the movement against the Islamic Republic is far more diverse — and more divided. Understanding who else is fighting for Iran's future is essential to understanding whether a post-regime transition is possible.

Woman Life Freedom Movement

Born from the 2022 Mahsa Amini protests, this leaderless, horizontal feminist movement remains the emotional core of the uprising. The chant "Zan, Zendegi, Azadi" ("Woman, Life, Freedom") has become Iran's defining protest slogan — now heard from university campuses to Baluchestan. The movement explicitly rejects any single leader, including Pahlavi, and demands a secular democratic republic. Its decentralized nature makes it impossible for the regime to decapitate, but also difficult to translate into political power.

Congress of Nationalities of Federal Iran (CNFI)

A coalition representing Iran's ethnic minorities — Kurds, Baloch, Arabs, Azeris, and Turkmen — who together constitute roughly 50% of Iran's population. The CNFI advocates for a federal Iran where minority regions have autonomy over language, culture, and local governance. Their key condition for any transition: a constitutional guarantee of federal rights. Kurdish leader Mustafa Hijri has met with both Pahlavi and European officials. Their alignment with Pahlavi's "wide tent" is conditional — they want federalism written into any transition roadmap.

Left-Leaning Secularists & Labor Movement

Iran's labor unions — particularly in the oil sector, teachers, and truck drivers — have conducted repeated strikes since 2022. Many are aligned with socialist or social-democratic opposition groups that distrust both the monarchy and Western-backed transition plans. The Haft Tappeh Sugarcane Workers' Union and Teachers' Trade Association have been among the most persistent organized forces. Their demands are as much economic as political — they want redistribution of IRGC economic assets, not just regime change. They are skeptical of the Pahlavi-Kushner-Palm Beach coalition.

Mojahedin-e Khalq (MEK/MKO) — Controversial

The MEK was removed from US terrorist designation in 2012 and maintains a political arm (NCRI) in Paris. It claims to be the most organized opposition force. However, the MEK is deeply unpopular inside Iran due to its collaboration with Saddam Hussein during the Iran-Iraq War and its cult-like organizational structure. Most Iranian activists and diaspora groups explicitly reject MEK participation in any transition. Their continued presence in the opposition coalition is a source of significant internal conflict.

The Diaspora Factor: 5 Million Iranians Abroad

The Iranian diaspora — concentrated in Los Angeles ("Tehrangeles"), Toronto, London, Paris, and Stockholm — has emerged as a critical financial and political support network for the protest movement. Diaspora Iranians fund VPN access, satellite internet equipment, and international advocacy campaigns. The Feb 14 Munich day of action drew tens of thousands across multiple continents. However, diaspora politics suffer from the same fractures as the in-country opposition: monarchists, republicans, leftists, feminists, and ethnic groups all compete for international legitimacy. The Jared Kushner–Palm Beach meetings (Feb 5) with Iranian-American business leaders suggest the Trump administration is specifically cultivating the pro-market, pro-Western wing of the diaspora as its preferred transition vehicle.

The Unity Problem — Can They Work Together?

The history of Iranian opposition in exile is one of spectacular infighting. The 2022-2023 uprising failed in part because the opposition could not agree on a unified leadership or platform. The current situation may be different — the scale of the massacre and the breadth of the protests have created unprecedented common ground. But significant fault lines remain: Pahlavi's critics accuse him of marginalizing other voices; the MEK's presence poisons any coalition it joins; Kurdish federalists cannot accept a unitary state; and the Women Life Freedom movement refuses hierarchical leadership on principle. A successful transition requires solving a political coordination problem that has defeated Iranian dissidents for four decades.
