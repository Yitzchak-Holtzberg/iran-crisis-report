Inside Iran: A Regime Under Siege

Seven converging crises threatening the Islamic Republic
Critical

☠ Crisis 1: The January Massacre

What began as economic protests on December 28, 2025 — driven by 40%+ general inflation and 70% food price inflation — escalated into the largest anti-government uprising in the Islamic Republic's history. On January 8-9, 2026, Khamenei ordered security forces to fire on protesters with live ammunition, tear gas, and prohibited weapons. What followed was the deadliest two-day protest massacre in modern history.

Death toll estimates vary enormously: the Iranian government acknowledged 3,117 deaths; HRANA documented 7,015 confirmed deaths (6,508 protesters); Iran International's investigation found evidence of 36,500+ killed, including 209 government-affiliated personnel.

Amnesty International Alert
At least 30 people, including several children, are at risk of execution in connection with the uprising. Several were under 18 at the time. Torture methods documented include: threats of sexual assault, mock executions, beatings with cables, burning with irons, and nail extraction.

✊ Crisis 2: The Student Uprising (Feb 21-25)

As universities reopened, students organized 40-day memorial protests for those killed — a deeply significant tradition in Shia Islam marking the end of mourning. Starting Feb. 21, protests erupted at University of Tehran, Sharif University of Technology, Amirkabir University, Shahid Beheshti University, and campuses in Isfahan and Mashhad — now spreading to all 31 provinces.

By day five (today), demonstrations continue despite the regime's "red line" warnings, with class boycotts and campus occupations. Students chant "Death to the dictator," "Woman, life, freedom," and — significantly — chants calling for Reza Pahlavi to replace Khamenei. On Feb. 24, the Sharif University of Technology website was hacked, its name reverted to pre-revolution "Aryamehr University." A separate cyberattack sent 50,000 Iranians a text message: "To the oppressed people of Iran: the US president is a man of action, you will soon see." Iran's cyberpolice is investigating. Government spokesperson Mohajerani confirmed many universities remain closed, with some already moving classes fully online for the rest of the Iranian calendar year (ending March 20).

"We will speak with the protesters but... rioters should be put in their place."
— Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, attempting to split the movement

📈 Crisis 3: Economic Freefall

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent admitted Washington deliberately engineered a dollar shortage to trigger runaway inflation. Testifying before the Senate Banking Committee, Bessent described the trigger event explicitly: "It came to a swift and, I would say, grand culmination in December, when one of the largest banks in Iran went under. There was a run on the bank. The central bank had to print money. The Iranian currency went into free fall, inflation exploded and hence we have seen the Iranian people out on the street." The rial has collapsed from 42,000 to over 1.47 million per dollar — effectively rendering purchasing power non-existent for imports.

The Patronage Crisis

The IRGC's loyalty has always been sustained by economic patronage — access to smuggling networks, construction contracts, import monopolies. With oil revenue collapsing and sanctions tightening, the regime can no longer afford to buy loyalty. This is why the IRGC power struggle (below) has become existential: it's not just about ideology, it's about shrinking revenue to divide among competing factions.

🔒 Crisis 4: The Digital Iron Curtain

On January 8, Iran imposed a near-total internet shutdown — the longest ever recorded in the country — cutting off 92 million citizens from the global web. Even phone and text messaging were restricted.

Although partially relaxed on January 28, the regime is now building something far more sinister: a two-tier internet system where only security-vetted elites receive global access, while 90 million citizens are locked inside a state-controlled intranet. Government spokesperson Fatemeh Mohajerani confirmed international access won't be restored until at least late March.

Foreign Policy — Feb 24, 2026
"The regime is testing a two-tier internet where access becomes a vetted privilege... building a system that grants web access only to security-vetted elites, while locking 90 million citizens inside an intranet."

Georgia Tech researchers confirmed the blackout extended to Starlink satellite terminals, which Iran has been jamming. The shutdown is simultaneously concealing ongoing atrocities and destroying the economy — Iranian businesses report catastrophic losses from weeks without connectivity.

The CIA released a Farsi-language video on X, Instagram, and YouTube offering detailed instructions for Iranian insiders to make secure contact: use a paid VPN registered outside Iran/Russia/China, a disposable device, private/incognito browsing, and access a dedicated Tor (.onion) portal. CIA Director Ratcliffe noted previous campaigns targeting Russian and Chinese speakers had yielded actionable results. Iran condemned the campaign as open interference. 🇧🇭 Crisis 5: The Ethnic Periphery Erupts

While students protest in Tehran, Iran's ethnic minorities have borne the most brutal repression. The regime deployed heavy machine guns, armored vehicles, tanks, helicopter gunships in Kurdish, Baloch, and Arab-majority regions — a dramatically heavier response than in Persian cities.

Kurdistan

2,000+ Kurdish citizens arrested. Families report torture: threats of sexual assault, mock executions, beatings with cables, burning with irons, nail extraction. IRGC and Basij conducting warrantless raids and mass detentions.

Sistan-Baluchestan

Heavy military deployment with reports of armored vehicles and helicopter gunships used against civilians. The Baloch community, already among Iran's most marginalized, faces the most militarized crackdown.

Khuzestan (Arab)

Ahwaz Arabs face heavy artillery and military-grade suppression. The oil-rich province has long been a site of ethnic grievance, with communities demanding linguistic and cultural rights.

Azerbaijan (Turkish)

Ethnic Azeri Turks — Iran's largest minority — have joined the broader protest movement. The Congress of Nationalities of Federal Iran now represents all minority groups united against the regime.

💧 Crisis 6: The Water Catastrophe

Iran faces a water crisis of existential proportions that predates the current political upheaval — but now accelerates it. Decades of IRGC-directed dam-building, agricultural mismanagement, and climate change have turned one of the ancient world's most water-rich civilizations into a country running dry.

Lake Urmia, once the Middle East's largest lake (5,200 km²) and a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve, has shrunk to less than 10% of its former volume. Its exposed lakebed now creates toxic salt and dust storms carrying heavy metals across northwestern Iran, causing respiratory disease, crop failure, and mass displacement.

Root Cause: IRGC Dam Policy

The IRGC and its affiliated companies built 600+ dams across Iran — many without environmental review — diverting rivers that fed lakes and wetlands. The Zayandeh River that sustained Isfahan (pop. 2M) now runs dry for months each year. Farmers in Khuzestan (Crisis 5's Arab heartland) have seen the Karun River — Iran's largest — reduced to a trickle, fueling both the ethnic and water crises simultaneously.

Tehran itself (pop. 9M metro) faces acute water rationing. The Karaj Dam reservoir is at historic lows. Officials have warned of a "Day Zero" scenario — the point at which municipal taps stop flowing — as early as summer 2026. Meanwhile, Iranian agriculture consumes 92% of available water, much of it for IRGC-controlled pistachio and saffron export farms that prioritize foreign currency over domestic food supply.

The water crisis is now inseparable from the protest movement. The December 2025 economic protests were partly driven by water shortages in Isfahan, Khuzestan, and Sistan-Baluchestan. When the regime shut off internet access in January, it also shut off the water-monitoring apps that farmers relied on, leading to further crop losses.

🕵 Crisis 7: Axis of Resistance Unraveling

Iran's carefully built "Axis of Resistance" — its network of proxy forces across the Middle East — has suffered catastrophic losses since mid-2025. What was once Tehran's primary tool of asymmetric power projection is now fractured, depleted, and increasingly unreliable.

Hezbollah — Decimated

Hassan Nasrallah killed (Sep 2025) along with most of the senior command council. Israel's June-August 2025 twelve-day campaign destroyed 60%+ of Hezbollah's long-range missile arsenal. The Syria supply route — Iran's land bridge — has been severed since the fall of Assad in Dec 2024. Hezbollah is now a regional militia, not a strategic deterrent.

Houthis — Restructuring

Yemen's Ansar Allah has proven the most resilient proxy, still launching anti-shipping missiles into the Red Sea. But US/UK strikes since Jan 2024 have degraded their command infrastructure. Increasingly operating independently from Tehran — a sign of weakening control, not strength.

Iraqi PMF / Kataib Hezbollah

Iraqi Shia militias remain Iran's most intact proxy force. Kataib Hezbollah issued a Jan 2026 statement calling for "martyrdom operations" against US forces in Iraq and Syria. However, the Iraqi government is increasingly distancing itself, and US strikes on PMF positions have degraded coordination.

Syria — Collapsed

The fall of Assad (Dec 2024) eliminated Iran's most critical regional foothold. Syrian proxies — Liwa Fatemiyoun, NDF units, IRGC advisors — have been expelled or disbanded. The "land bridge" from Tehran to Beirut no longer exists. Iran lost billions in infrastructure investment overnight.

Strategic Implication

The collapse of the Axis of Resistance means Iran's primary deterrence strategy has failed. Tehran built this network specifically to threaten retaliation against US and Israeli targets without direct state-to-state warfare. With Hezbollah's leadership wiped out, Syria lost, and the land bridge severed, Iran must now either escalate to direct confrontation (which its degraded military cannot sustain) or accept a significantly weakened negotiating position in Geneva.

⚔ The IRGC Power Struggle

The severe repression has deepened internal rifts within Iran's security structure. Reports indicate growing moral and psychological strain on some Basij and provincial IRGC units — soldiers ordered to fire on their own people.

A recent IRGC command reshuffle, combined with public attacks among political factions and repeated calls for "unity," points to a governing system increasingly unable to maintain its own cohesion. The convergence of:

  • Internal IRGC command changes
  • Public factional infighting between hardliners and pragmatists
  • External military threat from the US
  • Economic collapse eroding the patronage networks that keep the regime loyal

...creates what analysts at the National Interest describe as a potential "Day After Khamenei" scenario where the 86-year-old Supreme Leader's inevitable succession becomes an IRGC power struggle rather than a democratic transition.

Reza Pahlavi & the Opposition

The exiled prince, the transition roadmap, and the fractured dream of a free Iran

The Man in Exile

Reza Pahlavi, 65, eldest son of Iran's last Shah, has emerged as the most prominent figure in the Iranian opposition — and the most controversial. Based in the Washington, D.C. area, he has positioned himself as a transitional leader rather than a monarch-in-waiting.

  • Explicitly rejects restoring the monarchy — advocates secular democracy
  • Has direct communication channel with the Trump administration

The Broader Opposition Landscape

Pahlavi is the most visible face of the Iranian opposition, but the movement against the Islamic Republic is far more diverse — and more divided. Understanding who else is fighting for Iran's future is essential to understanding whether a post-regime transition is possible.

Woman Life Freedom Movement

Born from the 2022 Mahsa Amini protests, this leaderless, horizontal feminist movement remains the emotional core of the uprising. The chant "Zan, Zendegi, Azadi" ("Woman, Life, Freedom") has become Iran's defining protest slogan — now heard from university campuses to Baluchestan. The movement explicitly rejects any single leader, including Pahlavi, and demands a secular democratic republic. Its decentralized nature makes it impossible for the regime to decapitate, but also difficult to translate into political power.

Congress of Nationalities of Federal Iran (CNFI)

A coalition representing Iran's ethnic minorities — Kurds, Baloch, Arabs, Azeris, and Turkmen — who together constitute roughly 50% of Iran's population. The CNFI advocates for a federal Iran where minority regions have autonomy over language, culture, and local governance. Their key condition for any transition: a constitutional guarantee of federal rights. Kurdish leader Mustafa Hijri has met with both Pahlavi and European officials. Their alignment with Pahlavi's "wide tent" is conditional — they want federalism written into any transition roadmap.

Left-Leaning Secularists & Labor Movement

Iran's labor unions — particularly in the oil sector, teachers, and truck drivers — have conducted repeated strikes since 2022. Many are aligned with socialist or social-democratic opposition groups that distrust both the monarchy and Western-backed transition plans. The Haft Tappeh Sugarcane Workers' Union and Teachers' Trade Association have been among the most persistent organized forces. Their demands are as much economic as political — they want redistribution of IRGC economic assets, not just regime change. They are skeptical of the Pahlavi-Kushner-Palm Beach coalition.

Mojahedin-e Khalq (MEK/MKO) — Controversial

The MEK was removed from US terrorist designation in 2012 and maintains a political arm (NCRI) in Paris. It claims to be the most organized opposition force. However, the MEK is deeply unpopular inside Iran due to its collaboration with Saddam Hussein during the Iran-Iraq War and its cult-like organizational structure. Most Iranian activists and diaspora groups explicitly reject MEK participation in any transition. Their continued presence in the opposition coalition is a source of significant internal conflict.

The Diaspora Factor: 5 Million Iranians Abroad

The Iranian diaspora — concentrated in Los Angeles ("Tehrangeles"), Toronto, London, Paris, and Stockholm — has emerged as a critical financial and political support network for the protest movement. Diaspora Iranians fund VPN access, satellite internet equipment, and international advocacy campaigns. The Feb 14 Munich day of action drew tens of thousands across multiple continents. However, diaspora politics suffer from the same fractures as the in-country opposition: monarchists, republicans, leftists, feminists, and ethnic groups all compete for international legitimacy. The Jared Kushner–Palm Beach meetings (Feb 5) with Iranian-American business leaders suggest the Trump administration is specifically cultivating the pro-market, pro-Western wing of the diaspora as its preferred transition vehicle.

The Unity Problem — Can They Work Together?

The history of Iranian opposition in exile is one of spectacular infighting. The 2022-2023 uprising failed in part because the opposition could not agree on a unified leadership or platform. The current situation may be different — the scale of the massacre and the breadth of the protests have created unprecedented common ground. But significant fault lines remain: Pahlavi's critics accuse him of marginalizing other voices; the MEK's presence poisons any coalition it joins; Kurdish federalists cannot accept a unitary state; and the Women Life Freedom movement refuses hierarchical leadership on principle. A successful transition requires solving a political coordination problem that has defeated Iranian dissidents for four decades.

What a Deal Would Actually Require

The gap between US demands and Iranian red lines is significant — but not unbridgeable in theory. What the two sides are actually negotiating:

US Demands (Trump's Red Lines)

  • Zero enrichment — or at most 3.67% (JCPOA 1.0 level), far below weapons-grade
  • Dismantlement of Fordow, Natanz advanced centrifuge cascades, and the "Pickaxe Mountain" facility
  • Intrusive IAEA inspections — anywhere, anytime access, including military sites like Parchin
  • End to ballistic missile development beyond defensive range
  • Cessation of proxy funding — Hezbollah, Houthis, PMF
  • Trump stated in SOTU: "I will never allow the world's number one sponsor of terror to have a nuclear weapon" — implying regime behavior change, not just nuclear limits

Iran's Red Lines (Non-Negotiable)

  • Right to enrichment — Khamenei calls this an "unalienable right"; Araghchi: "Iran will under no circumstances forgo its right to peaceful nuclear technology"
  • No military site inspections — Parchin is a military facility; IAEA access would be unprecedented
  • Sanctions lifted upfront, not in phases — Iran was burned by JCPOA 1.0 when the US withdrew in 2018 after Iran complied
  • No interference in regional policy — proxy support framed as "resistance" ideology, not negotiable
  • Parliament Speaker Ghalibaf: "If you choose the path of diplomacy — one in which Iranian dignity and mutual interests are respected — we will remain at the table."
The Potential Bridging Formula

Diplomatic sources suggest the possible middle ground: Iran caps enrichment at 20% (not zero, but far below 90% weapons-grade), accepts IAEA cameras at all declared sites (but not military), ships its 60-90% enriched uranium stockpile to a third country (Russia or Turkey), and agrees to a 10-year moratorium on advanced centrifuge deployment. In exchange: partial sanctions relief, unfreezing of $6B in South Korean oil payments, and a US commitment not to seek regime change. Whether Khamenei — and Trump — can sell this to their domestic audiences is the central question.

The UK-US Rift

PM Starmer blocked the US from using RAF Fairford (B-52/B-2 bombers) and Diego Garcia. Legal basis: participation would breach international law. In retaliation, Trump withdrew support for the UK-Mauritius Chagos Islands deal.

However, Britain quietly deployed Typhoons and F-35Bs to Qatar and Cyprus — defensive assets in case of Iranian retaliation. UK officials privately express concern that a US strike without British legal cover sets a dangerous precedent for unilateral military action.

The practical consequence: without Diego Garcia, B-2 Spirit stealth bombers carrying GBU-57 Massive Ordnance Penetrators — the only weapons that can potentially reach the deepest Iranian bunkers — would require either a 34-hour round-trip from Whiteman Air Force Base, Missouri, or a diplomatic reversal from Starmer. Senior US military planners have quietly noted the strike package is "less effective" without the Indian Ocean base.

Israel: Finger on the Trigger

IDF at highest readiness since the June 2025 twelve-day war. 280,000 reservists authorized. IDF Chief: "prepared to employ unprecedented offensive power." Coordinating with CENTCOM; requesting advance warning before any US strikes. Jan 5 Security Cabinet strike authorization remains active.

Israel's position is uniquely urgent: Iran's ballistic missile arsenal — even degraded from the June 2025 strikes — can still reach Tel Aviv in under 12 minutes. The "Pickaxe Mountain" facility, if it produces a nuclear device, changes Israel's strategic calculus permanently. Prime Minister Netanyahu has reportedly told the Security Cabinet: "We cannot allow a second chance."

Israel's independent strike option: the IAF's F-35I "Adir" fleet has demonstrated the range to reach Iran via Saudi airspace (with or without Riyadh's permission). The June 2025 strikes proved Israeli aircraft can penetrate Iranian air defenses. But without American GBU-57 bunker-busters, Israel cannot guarantee destroying the deepest facilities — making US participation a strategic necessity, not just a preference.

Scenario 1

Diplomatic Deal — JCPOA 3.0

Likelihood: 15%

Iran agrees in Geneva (or in subsequent rounds) to cap uranium enrichment at 3.67% — well below weapons-grade — and accept intrusive IAEA inspections. In exchange, the US lifts sanctions in phases and Trump declares a diplomatic victory.

What Would Trigger This

  • Khamenei quietly authorizes FM Araghchi to accept enrichment limits
  • Trump frames concessions as Iran "surrendering" — gives Khamenei political cover to call it a "resistance win"
  • Saudi Arabia and UAE pressure Iran privately to take the deal
  • IRGC hardliners are overruled — or Khamenei health scare concentrates decision-making

Why It's Unlikely

  • Khamenei has called enrichment an "unalienable right" for 20 years — any capitulation looks like regime defeat

If It Happens: Consequences

Oil markets immediately stabilize — WTI drops back to $55-60. Strait of Hormuz tensions ease. US military begins draw-down within 30 days. But: the protest movement inside Iran remains unaddressed. A deal with the regime — not the Iranian people — could delay but not prevent eventual political rupture. Pahlavi and opposition groups would likely oppose any deal that leaves Khamenei in power. The regime's survival would be extended, not resolved.

The Key Unknown: Can the US Destroy "Pickaxe Mountain"?

Iran's new deep centrifuge facility south of Natanz may be buried at 80+ meters underground — beyond the GBU-57 Massive Ordnance Penetrator's effective penetration depth of ~60 meters in hardened rock. If the facility survives the initial strike, Iran could resume 90% enrichment within months. The Parchin nuclear site's new concrete-and-soil shield (confirmed Feb 20) is a direct countermeasure to this threat. Military planners have no confirmed answer to whether the strike can be decisive.

Global Economic Consequences

Goldman Sachs estimates Hormuz closure pushes WTI above $100/barrel within days. Every $10 increase costs the US economy ~$70B annually. Global recession risk: High. Immediate effects: European energy shock (LNG rerouting from Qatar impossible overnight), Indian and Japanese industrial slowdowns, Chinese manufacturing disruption. The global economy has no strategic petroleum reserve large enough to absorb a prolonged Hormuz closure.

Scenario 3

Regime Collapse — Revolution From Within

Likelihood: 20%

The convergence of seven simultaneous crises — economic freefall, mass protests, military pressure, water catastrophe, ethnic uprising, proxy collapse, and IRGC fracturing — passes a threshold beyond the regime's ability to manage. The Islamic Republic fractures from within before a shot is fired.

The Tipping Point Conditions

  • Khamenei death or incapacitation — the 86-year-old Supreme Leader's inevitable succession triggers an IRGC power struggle with no clear heir
  • IRGC unit defections — economic privation and moral strain from firing on civilians causes provincial units to stand down
  • Banking system collapse — if a second major bank fails (as threatened), hyperinflation accelerates beyond control
  • Generals accept Pahlavi's "wide tent" — IRGC insiders calculate regime survival is impossible and negotiate a soft landing
  • Protests reach critical mass — student uprising expands to include workers, bazaar merchants, and ultimately the IRGC's own families

The Transition Problem

  • No single opposition leader commands national consensus — Pahlavi is the most prominent but deeply divisive
  • IRGC controls 60-70% of the economy — any transition must deal with this entrenched power structure
  • Nuclear material security — 1,000+ kg of 60-90% enriched uranium must be secured immediately to prevent proliferation
  • Ethnic fracture risk — Kurdish, Baloch, Arab, and Azeri minorities may push for autonomy or secession in a power vacuum
  • Historical precedent: Libya 2011, Iraq 2003 — externally-supported regime changes typically produce prolonged civil conflict
The CIA's Play: Accelerating This Scenario

The CIA's Feb 24-25 Farsi-language recruitment video — offering IRGC insiders a secure channel to defect — is a direct play for Scenario 3. By specifically targeting insiders with access to nuclear, military, and financial information, the Agency is simultaneously collecting intelligence and signaling to IRGC officers that there is a path out. If senior IRGC commanders begin passing secrets — or, crucially, standing down during protests — the collapse scenario becomes self-fulfilling. The "wide tent" offer from Pahlavi and the CIA recruitment effort are complementary operations targeting the same audience: the 20,000+ senior IRGC officers who must decide which side of history they want to be on.

Scenario 4

Prolonged Standoff — Sanctions & Attrition

Likelihood: 10%

Geneva produces a partial framework — not a full deal, but enough to delay Trump's trigger finger. Both sides declare partial victory. The military build-up remains in place, sanctions continue, and Iran continues nuclear reconstruction while talks drag on. The crisis becomes a "frozen conflict" — an unresolved standoff that neither side is willing to end decisively.

Why This Is Least Likely

Trump has personally staked his credibility on the "10-day" ultimatum. A prolonged standoff would require him to publicly back down — something his political brand makes nearly impossible. Iran, meanwhile, cannot afford to keep its military on high alert and its economy in free fall indefinitely. The economics alone force a resolution within weeks. Iran's foreign exchange reserves are estimated at under $10B accessible (the rest is frozen under US sanctions, per OFAC estimates and Congressional Research Service reporting), meaning the current burn rate makes a standoff untenable beyond April 2026.
