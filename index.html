UK DEFENSE — BRITAIN OFFERS DEFENSIVE MILITARY ASSISTANCE TO GULF ALLIES
The UK Ministry of Defence announced Britain is offering defensive military assistance to Gulf allies. Specific offers include the Sky Sabre (CAMM) short-to-medium range air defense system, Typhoon jets in defensive CAP roles, and additional Royal Navy assets for humanitarian corridor protection. HMS Diamond (Type 45 destroyer), already in the region with advanced air defense capability, has been formally forward-deployed to support Gulf air defense. The UK stressed these are defensive, not offensive commitments, consistent with PM Starmer's refusal to grant the US access to RAF bases for strikes. — Source: UK MoD, Reuters, Mar 1

Germany also reconfirmed its €50M humanitarian pledge and its support for a UN humanitarian corridor into Iran. — Source: German MoD, German Foreign Ministry, Mar 1

CSIS

Center for Strategic & International Studies

📋 Operation Epic Fury and the Remnants of Iran's Nuclear Program (Feb 28)

CSIS concludes that strikes have degraded but not eliminated Iran's nuclear capability. Key findings: Natanz enrichment hall surface structures destroyed; Fordow underground facility struck, penetration depth disputed; Isfahan research reactor damaged; Pickaxe Mountain facility south of Natanz — BDA inconclusive. CSIS assesses Iran retains 600–900 kg of 20–60% enriched uranium at dispersed sites, and can reconstitute 90% enrichment capability within 6–18 months if political authority survives. CSIS recommends a verified "zero enrichment" deal within the next 30-day window as the only path that forecloses a Phase III campaign. — Source: CSIS, Feb 28, 2026

⚓ US Military in the Middle East: The Numbers Behind Trump's Threats (Feb 24)

CSIS pre-strike force assessment: three carrier strike groups, 150+ aircraft surged, 35,000+ troops positioned — CSIS described this as "the largest naval force in the region since Operation Iraqi Freedom (2003)." CSIS analysts noted the force is sized for punitive strikes, not sustained occupation, and lacks the ground component for extended campaign logistics. Post-strike, this assessment underpins Phase II questions: Can the US sustain a second strike wave? CSIS says yes — for 7–10 days of high-intensity strikes before carrier strike groups require replenishment cycles. — Source: CSIS, Feb 24, 2026

CSIS Phase II Outlook — Key Questions

  • Nuclear mission completion: BDA on Pickaxe Mountain and Fordow underground centrifuge halls within 72 hours will determine whether Phase III strikes are politically necessary
  • Succession vacuum: CSIS identifies the 48–72 hour window after Khamenei's death as a decisive diplomatic opening — the only period before IRGC factions consolidate a new authority structure
  • Hormuz closure risk: CSIS models a 14-day closure driving WTI above $120/barrel; 30-day closure triggering global recession conditions. US strategic petroleum reserves provide 30–45 days of buffer
  • Phase III trigger: If enrichment capability survives at Pickaxe Mountain, CSIS assesses Trump administration faces binary choice — accept a nuclear-capable Iran or launch a third strike wave within 30 days

CSIS Policy Recommendations (Post-Strike)

  • Open a ceasefire channel immediately — the 48-hour succession window is the only period where a political deal can be struck without a permanent counterpart; Oman is the only credible intermediary
  • Secure enriched uranium stockpiles — CSIS calls for pre-positioning IAEA rapid-response teams within 24 hours of regime fracture to prevent nuclear material from becoming a bargaining chip or proliferation vector
  • Do not publicly commit to regime change — CSIS analysts warn that explicit US embrace of Pahlavi as transitional authority may consolidate IRGC resistance rather than fracture it
  • Gulf state economic guarantees — Saudi and UAE reconstruction pledges are the financial foundation of any managed transition; CSIS recommends US facilitate Gulf financial commitment before any recognition of transitional authority

Carnegie

Carnegie Endowment for International Peace

Carnegie's Karim Sadjadpour, the leading US analyst of Iranian domestic politics, frames the post-Khamenei succession as "the most dangerous transition in Middle Eastern politics since 1979." Carnegie's analysis prioritizes the Mojtaba Khamenei variable — describing him as a figure who was "systematically positioned to inherit his father's mantle" but who faces a legitimacy deficit that may prove fatal to the junta scenario.

The Succession Analysis (Sadjadpour)

  • Mojtaba's legitimacy problem: He is not a senior cleric and cannot claim the velayat-e faqih (guardianship of the jurist) that underpins the Islamic Republic's constitutional legitimacy; any attempt to rule would be transparently dynastic — the opposite of the 1979 revolution's founding ideology
  • Assembly of Experts: The constitutional body responsible for selecting a new Supreme Leader is 88 members, median age 72, and split between hardliners and pragmatists. Carnegie assesses Mojtaba can secure a slim majority but not the two-thirds needed for a politically legitimate selection
  • The reformist wildcard: Carnegie identifies a small bloc of reformist clerics inside the Assembly who may see the post-strike chaos as their last opportunity to assert influence; their alignment could determine whether the succession produces a junta or a compromise figure

Carnegie: Democratic Transition Conditions

  • Five simultaneous conditions now exist that have never previously been true: (1) IRGC economic base under destruction; (2) protest movement has a named leader for the first time in 46 years; (3) CIA operational defection channel is live; (4) Trump administration back-channel to Pahlavi confirmed; (5) Khamenei dead, removing the authority figure who could order unified resistance
  • The conversion problem: Carnegie warns that conditions ≠ outcome — Iran's opposition has had favorable conditions before (2009, 2019) and failed to convert them; the difference this time is the external military dimension, which changes the IRGC's internal calculation
  • Kurdish fracture as spoiler: Carnegie assesses the Pahlavi–Kurdish dissident split as the single most likely obstacle to a coordinated transition; PJAK in the northwest could open a low-grade insurgency within days of any transitional authority announcement
Brookings

Brookings Institution

The Day-After Problem

Brookings' Iran analysts draw the sharpest historical parallel: Iraq 2003. The US dismantled a state apparatus in 21 days and spent 20 years managing the consequences. Iran has four times Iraq's population, an intact nuclear program, and a protest movement that has named an external leader rather than emerged organically from within. Brookings flags three critical planning failures it assesses the US has not addressed: (1) no post-strike civil administration plan for 31 provinces with IRGC-dominated economies; (2) nuclear material chain-of-custody gap — 1,000+ kg of enriched uranium at dispersed sites with no US/IAEA pre-positioned rapid-response teams; (3) economic reconstitution financing — $100B+ in frozen assets cannot be unlocked without Congressional action and EU agreement that have not yet been initiated. Brookings does not assess that military strikes alone produce a stable outcome without these three elements.

Atlantic Council

The following assessment reflects confirmed and reported strike effects from Day 1 (Feb 28) through Day 2 (Mar 1). BDA in active strike environments is inherently uncertain; the Pentagon has not issued a full public accounting. Independent verification of specific targets remains limited due to Iranian communications disruptions.

⚠️ The Command Vacuum: Iran Without Khamenei

Khamenei's death removes the singular authority that has controlled Iran's nuclear trigger, proxy activation orders, and all strategic military decisions for 35 years. This is not merely a symbolic loss — it creates a genuine and dangerous command vacuum over a still-armed state with ballistic missiles, drones, and an intact naval force in the world's most critical energy chokepoint.

Military Risks: Decapitation Without Disarmament

  • Iran retains an estimated 1,000–1,300 ballistic missiles after the ~170-missile first salvo — with no single confirmed authority now controlling their release
  • IRGC corps commanders may hold pre-delegated launch authority for second-wave ballistic salvos — a "dead hand" scenario where tactical commanders fire without political oversight
  • The Quds Force, absent central command, may activate terrorist sleeper cells globally under pre-standing contingency orders — no authorization needed if Khamenei is confirmed dead
  • Hezbollah, Houthis, and Iraqi PMF forces may escalate independently — the decapitation of the Quds Force removes the coordinating authority but not the proxy arsenals
  • Risk of competing IRGC factions forming separate power centers — creating unpredictable, uncoordinated decision-making loops with no diplomatic interlocutor
  • Iran's cyber units (APT33/APT34) operate autonomously and may launch pre-planned destructive attacks against Gulf financial systems and energy infrastructure without central authorization

Succession Under Fire: Who Controls Iran Now?

  • Assembly of Experts: Constitutionally empowered to elect a new Supreme Leader — but the body is fragmented, some members may be dead or unreachable, and it has never convened under active air strikes
  • Mojtaba Khamenei (son): Had been discreetly groomed as a potential successor but holds no formal military, clerical, or governmental authority — IRGC generals are unlikely to defer to him
  • IRGC General Command Council: The most powerful surviving military body — may assert de facto control over ballistic missiles and strategic assets regardless of formal succession processes
  • President Masoud Pezeshkian: Reformist president constitutionally subordinate to the Supreme Leader — elevated in interregnum, but commands no IRGC loyalty and has no control over the missile force
  • Expediency Council / Judiciary: Could attempt to convene an interim governance structure — but both lack the IRGC's weapons and the IRGC's willingness to follow civilian orders
  • The Hardliner-Reformist Fault Line: The succession fight between IRGC hardliners and regime reformists could fracture what remains of Iranian state cohesion at the worst possible moment
The Most Dangerous 72 Hours

The window between Khamenei's death and the emergence of any successor authority is assessed by defense analysts as the most dangerous phase of the conflict. Pre-delegated launch protocols may allow IRGC corps commanders to fire second-wave ballistic salvos without political authorization — potentially larger than the 170-missile first wave. US and Israeli military planners are almost certainly accelerating strikes against remaining launch infrastructure before any successor can consolidate control and either escalate further or negotiate a ceasefire. The absence of a recognized decision-maker on the Iranian side also complicates any US back-channel ceasefire outreach.

The cumulative effect of the June 2025 Israeli-US strikes and the Feb 28–Mar 1 Operation Epic Fury strikes has fundamentally altered Iran's military posture. Iran is no longer a pre-strike power deterring an attack — it is a post-strike state that has fired its first salvo and is now assessing what remains. The central military question is no longer whether Iran will retaliate, but what authority, and with what remaining arsenal, Iran can mount a second wave.

Whether China continues supply into an active wartime Iran remains a critical open question — and a major source of US-China tension.

💥 Iran's Retaliation — Who Was Attacked & How (Feb 28–Mar 1)

Within hours of the Operation Epic Fury strikes, Iran activated its pre-planned asymmetric retaliation doctrine. The response was simultaneous and multi-front — approximately 170 ballistic missiles in coordinated salvos across five theaters, triggering the largest simultaneous air defense activation in regional history.

Summary — Iran's First Retaliatory Salvo (Feb 28–Mar 1)

Iran fired approximately ~170 ballistic missiles — the largest single Iranian missile barrage ever recorded — toward Israel and toward US military installations across four Gulf nations. Explosions were reported in Qatar, Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE. Air defense systems — Patriot PAC-3, THAAD, Arrow 3, Iron Dome, David's Sling, and Aegis BMD — were activated simultaneously across all theaters. As of March 2, 2026, the US-Israeli strikes have entered a second day while Iran has not launched a confirmed second ballistic-missile wave — though IRGC doctrine calls for a follow-on salvo 24–48 hours after an initial barrage to target repair crews and overwhelm reconstituted air defenses.

The Broader Opposition Landscape

Pahlavi is the most visible face of the Iranian opposition, but the movement against the Islamic Republic is far more diverse — and more divided. Understanding who else is fighting for Iran's future is essential to understanding whether a post-regime transition is possible.

Woman Life Freedom Movement

Born from the 2022 Mahsa Amini protests, this leaderless, horizontal feminist movement remains the emotional core of the uprising. The chant "Zan, Zendegi, Azadi" ("Woman, Life, Freedom") has become Iran's defining protest slogan — now heard from university campuses to Baluchestan. The movement explicitly rejects any single leader, including Pahlavi, and demands a secular democratic republic. Its decentralized nature makes it impossible for the regime to decapitate, but also difficult to translate into political power.

Congress of Nationalities of Federal Iran (CNFI)

A coalition representing Iran's ethnic minorities — Kurds, Baloch, Arabs, Azeris, and Turkmen — who together constitute roughly 50% of Iran's population. The CNFI advocates for a federal Iran where minority regions have autonomy over language, culture, and local governance. Their key condition for any transition: a constitutional guarantee of federal rights. Kurdish leader Mustafa Hijri has met with both Pahlavi and European officials. Their alignment with Pahlavi's "wide tent" is conditional — they want federalism written into any transition roadmap.

Left-Leaning Secularists & Labor Movement

Iran's labor unions — particularly in the oil sector, teachers, and truck drivers — have conducted repeated strikes since 2022. Many are aligned with socialist or social-democratic opposition groups that distrust both the monarchy and Western-backed transition plans. The Haft Tappeh Sugarcane Workers' Union and Teachers' Trade Association have been among the most persistent organized forces. Their demands are as much economic as political — they want redistribution of IRGC economic assets, not just regime change. They are skeptical of the Pahlavi-Kushner-Palm Beach coalition.

Mojahedin-e Khalq (MEK/MKO) — Controversial

The MEK was removed from US terrorist designation in 2012 and maintains a political arm (NCRI) in Paris. It claims to be the most organized opposition force. However, the MEK is deeply unpopular inside Iran due to its collaboration with Saddam Hussein during the Iran-Iraq War and its cult-like organizational structure. Most Iranian activists and diaspora groups explicitly reject MEK participation in any transition. Their continued presence in the opposition coalition is a source of significant internal conflict.

The Diaspora Factor: 5 Million Iranians Abroad

The Iranian diaspora — concentrated in Los Angeles ("Tehrangeles"), Toronto, London, Paris, and Stockholm — has emerged as a critical financial and political support network for the protest movement. Diaspora Iranians fund VPN access, satellite internet equipment, and international advocacy campaigns. The Feb 14 Munich day of action drew tens of thousands across multiple continents. However, diaspora politics suffer from the same fractures as the in-country opposition: monarchists, republicans, leftists, feminists, and ethnic groups all compete for international legitimacy. The Jared Kushner–Palm Beach meetings (Feb 5) with Iranian-American business leaders suggest the Trump administration is specifically cultivating the pro-market, pro-Western wing of the diaspora as its preferred transition vehicle.

The Unity Problem — Can They Work Together?

The history of Iranian opposition in exile is one of spectacular infighting. The 2022-2023 uprising failed in part because the opposition could not agree on a unified leadership or platform. The current situation may be different — the scale of the massacre and the breadth of the protests have created unprecedented common ground. But significant fault lines remain: Pahlavi's critics accuse him of marginalizing other voices; the MEK's presence poisons any coalition it joins; Kurdish federalists cannot accept a unitary state; and the Women Life Freedom movement refuses hierarchical leadership on principle. A successful transition requires solving a political coordination problem that has defeated Iranian dissidents for four decades.
