What confirmation would look like: a Tier 1–2 report (Reuters / AP) corroborating intelligence-to-intelligence contact; a US administration official confirming “back-channel communications are ongoing”; or a Vienna talks agenda including items only MOIS (not IAEA or diplomatic actors) could have tabled. This differs from the separate IRGC officer → CIA defection-channel report (Iran International, Tier-5, also unconfirmed) — that involves an individual defector, not the institutional intelligence service. — Sources: Iran International (Tier-5, opposition-aligned), Mar 3–4; Al Arabiya, Mar 4 — Tier-5 framing required; not confirmed by CENTCOM, State Dept, Reuters, or AP as of March 7, 2026

NUCLEAR — IRAN DENIES CIVILIAN NUCLEAR PROGRAMME TARGETED OR DESTROYED
Iranian officials acknowledged explosions at Fordow and Natanz on Mar 2 but officially characterise the strikes as hitting "military infrastructure" only — not Iran's civilian nuclear programme. This framing is contested: both Fordow and Natanz are IAEA-safeguarded civilian enrichment facilities under the NPT. Iran's position serves a dual purpose: domestically, it avoids admitting the nuclear programme was set back; internationally, it preserves legal standing under the NPT. US and Israeli officials describe the sites as weapons-relevant dual-use facilities. Independent verification of whether centrifuge enrichment capability has been destroyed is not possible without IAEA access, which Iran has not granted. — Status: Iranian claim contested by US, Israel, IAEA; independent verification blocked
NUCLEAR — RADIATION RELEASE FROM STRUCK ENRICHMENT SITES
Initial concerns that strikes on Fordow and Natanz might have caused a radiological release have not been borne out by current IAEA assessments. The watchdog stated that buildings at Natanz were damaged but "no radiological consequence [is] expected" based on available information. The IAEA is seeking access to both sites to complete verification; independent regional sensor data remains limited. — Sources: IAEA statement; Al Jazeera, Mar 3
LEADERSHIP — ASSEMBLY OF EXPERTS NAMES MOJTABA KHAMENEI NEW SUPREME LEADER LEGITIMACY CONTESTED
A partial session of the Assembly of Experts, convened under IRGC security escort, voted to name Mojtaba Khamenei as the new Supreme Leader on Mar 3, ending his 72-hour public absence. The vote is disputed on multiple grounds: Mojtaba holds no senior clerical rank or velayat-e faqih credentials; the quorum and independence of the session cannot be verified; numerous members were unreachable. Opposition figures including Reza Pahlavi and reformist clerics abroad have declared the appointment illegitimate. IRGC Commander Salami pledged immediate loyalty; the US and EU have not recognised the appointment. — Sources: Reuters, AP, Al Jazeera, Mar 3 — Appointment confirmed; legitimacy contested
LEADERSHIP — IRAN'S NEW LEADERSHIP COUNCIL LEGITIMACY CONTESTED
Following Khamenei's death, President Pezeshkian announced a three-man leadership council "has begun its work" on Mar 3, invoking constitutional succession procedures (Article 111). However, the mechanism's legitimacy is contested by multiple parties: the Assembly of Experts — the body constitutionally mandated to select a new Supreme Leader — has not publicly convened, with many members unreachable due to the active military situation and communications disruption. Iranian opposition figures abroad, including Reza Pahlavi, have rejected the council as an IRGC-backed manoeuvre. Several reformist clerics described the council as a caretaker mechanism only, not a permanent transfer of authority. The council's actual command authority over IRGC units in the field remains unclear. — Sources: AP, Mar 3; Reuters, Al Jazeera, Mar 3 — Legitimacy disputed
MARITIME — HORMUZ CLOSURE: IRGC CLAIM vs. OPERATIONAL REALITY
IRGC Commander publicly declared the Strait of Hormuz "closed" on Mar 2, with a threat to "set on fire" any ship attempting to pass. This declaration is contested in practice: the Strait remains technically navigable, with some vessels transiting under military escort or at high speed. The US Navy's 5th Fleet stated it "will not allow" Hormuz to be blocked and is conducting freedom-of-navigation patrols. While ~150 vessels are stranded and insurers have withdrawn cover, the strait is not physically obstructed. Iran's actual capability to close Hormuz to all traffic — as distinct from attacking individual vessels — is disputed by US naval analysts. — Sources: Reuters, Mar 2; USN 5th Fleet statement, Mar 2–3; maritime reality contested
TOTAL CASUALTY FIGURES — ALL SIDES DISPUTING NUMBERS
All sides are publishing divergent casualty figures for the conflict so far. Al Jazeera reported 555 total killed (Mar 2); Iran has not released an official figure and state media presents fragmentary, lower counts. US CENTCOM confirmed 6 US service members killed (CNBC/CBS, Mar 2) but has not addressed Iranian or civilian totals. Israel has not released its own figures. The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs stated it cannot verify casualty data "due to access restrictions and the fog of war." Human rights groups (HRANA, Iran International) are compiling reports but note severe information blackout conditions inside Iran. All figures should be treated as preliminary estimates subject to significant revision. — Status: All reported figures disputed; OCHA cannot verify; independent access denied

Carnegie scholar Eric Lob provides the most detailed analysis of the succession contest. The Assembly of Experts is moving to elect a new supreme leader to preserve regime continuity amid war, but faces extraordinary obstacles:

  • Israel bombed the Assembly's building in Qom during its first meeting on Tuesday and threatened to strike again if a successor is named
  • Trump insisted he is "going to choose" Iran's next leader — Reuters, Mar 5
  • Despite this, the Assembly appears determined to carry out its constitutional duty

Frontrunners:

  • Ayatollah Alireza Arafi — hardline cleric, Khamenei loyalist, Guardian Council member, head of Iran's seminaries; attained ayatollah rank with strong religious credentials
  • Mojtaba Khamenei — the late leader's son, with deep IRGC/Basij ties but no public office and only mid-ranking clerical status (Hojjat al-Eslam)

Lob concludes: "If the succession process can be carried out to its logical conclusion, it would probably bring a hardliner into power." Khamenei stacked the Assembly with hardliners through 2024 disqualifications; moderates like Rouhani and Hassan Khomeini are unlikely to be considered. — Source: Carnegie Endowment (Lob), Mar 6, 2026

Carnegie: Succession Dynamics (Mar 7)

  • Arafi — the institutional candidate: Ayatollah rank, 20+ publications, Guardian Council and Assembly of Experts member, head of seminaries; entered Assembly via Khamenei-arranged legal loophole in 2022 — signaling he was the preferred successor

RAND

RAND Corporation — Succession Scenarios & Strategic Risk

📈 Who — or What — Will Replace Iran's Supreme Leader? (RAND, Mar 2026)

RAND identifies four succession scenarios, noting this transition is fundamentally unlike the managed 1989 handoff — it occurs amid domestic unrest, economic crisis, and unprecedented military pressure:

  • Scenario 1 — "Digging In": A senior cleric approved by the Assembly maintains the existing system with minimal changes — the status quo continuation
  • Scenario 2 — "Cut and Run": Key leaders flee, as Assad did from Syria — low probability but not zero given the intensity of strikes
  • Scenario 3 — "Suppression and Succession": The regime becomes more reliant on the IRGC, producing a quasi-military junta with weaker clerical legitimacy — potentially more threatening as it stifles dissent while doubling down on resistance
  • Scenario 4 — Popular Uprising: The population succeeds in revolution — but it remains unclear who they would support and whether they could prevail without a military institution switching sides

The Basij base targeting campaign (ISW, Mar 7) directly enables Scenario 4 by degrading the regime's protest-suppression capacity. — Source: RAND, Mar 2026

RAND: Scenario Assessment — Week 2 Update (Mar 7)

  • Scenario 3 ("Suppression + Succession") is now the active track: Mojtaba/Arafi frontrunner status + IRGC consolidation + Pezeshkian as civilian front = quasi-junta in formation
  • Scenario 4 enablers strengthening: 9/23 Basij bases in Tehran destroyed; LEC HQs hit in Kurdish areas; currency collapse continuing — the regime's capacity to suppress popular unrest is materially degrading
  • Scenario 2 risk non-zero: As strikes expand to 3,000+ targets and the IRGC's claim of 6-month readiness is tested, elite defection calculus shifts
  • Combined force appears to be shaping toward Scenario 4: Targeting Basij/LEC while leaving civilian infrastructure (relatively) intact suggests a strategy of enabling popular uprising rather than destroying the state

Economic & Hormuz Risk — Week 2 (Mar 7)

  • Hormuz functionally closed without mines: Tanker traffic down 90%+; Iran declared strait closed and attacked ships; stopped short of mining — but the effect is the same (CFR, Mar 6)
  • Oil prices elevated but not at crisis levels yet: Iran's own exports halted; 20% of global oil/LNG transits Hormuz; Trump's DFC insurance/Navy escort offer is untested
  • IRGC 6-month claim vs. $891M/day US cost: Both sides face attrition — the question is which political system absorbs the cost better
  • Congressional pressure building: House narrowly rejected war-powers resolution; rising US casualties and costs will produce another vote
  • Cyber risk remains elevated: Iranian threat actors retain capability for critical-infrastructure targeting as kinetic pressure mounts

The Broader Opposition Landscape

Pahlavi is the most visible face of the Iranian opposition, but the movement against the Islamic Republic is far more diverse — and more divided. Four distinct forces are each fighting for Iran's future, each with incompatible visions of what that future looks like.

Born from the 2022 Mahsa Amini protests, this leaderless, horizontal feminist movement is the emotional core of the uprising. The chant "Zan, Zendegi, Azadi" is heard from university campuses to Baluchestan. The movement explicitly rejects any single leader — including Pahlavi — and demands a secular democratic republic. Its decentralized structure makes it impossible to decapitate, but difficult to translate into political power.

🌍 The Diaspora Factor: 5 Million Iranians Abroad

The Iranian diaspora — concentrated in Los Angeles ("Tehrangeles"), Toronto, London, Paris, and Stockholm — has emerged as a critical financial and political support network for the protest movement. Diaspora Iranians fund VPN access, satellite internet equipment, and international advocacy campaigns. The Feb 14 Munich day of action drew tens of thousands across multiple continents. However, diaspora politics suffer from the same fractures as the in-country opposition: monarchists, republicans, leftists, feminists, and ethnic groups all compete for international legitimacy. The Jared Kushner–Palm Beach meetings (Feb 5) suggest the Trump administration is specifically cultivating the pro-market, pro-Western wing of the diaspora as its preferred transition vehicle.

⚡ The Unity Problem — Can They Work Together?

The history of Iranian opposition in exile is one of spectacular infighting. The 2022–2023 uprising failed in part because the opposition could not agree on a unified leadership or platform. Fault lines remain sharp today: Pahlavi's critics accuse him of marginalizing other voices; the MEK's presence poisons any coalition it joins; Kurdish federalists cannot accept a unitary state; and the Women Life Freedom movement refuses hierarchical leadership on principle. A successful transition requires solving a political coordination problem that has defeated Iranian dissidents for four decades.
